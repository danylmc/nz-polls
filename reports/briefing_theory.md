# NZ Polling & Political Theory: Reference Briefing

**Scope:** Political theory applied to NZ polling evidence — scarcity, accountability, identity, institutional resilience (1993-2026)
**Data:** 1,020 party vote polls (1993-2026), 299 PM preference polls, NZES individual-level surveys (10 elections 1990-2023), Ipsos Issues Monitor (24 editions 2017-2025), Stats NZ GDP/CPI, RBNZ mortgage rates, MBIE petrol prices, OECD CCI/productivity/governance, BIS house prices, Freedom House ratings
**Date:** 9 March 2026
**Election:** 7 November 2026 (243 days away)

## Evidence Tiers

| Tier | Meaning | Criteria |
|------|---------|----------|
| **Confirmed** | Robust finding | p < 0.05, survives robustness checks, consistent across specifications |
| **Partial** | Directionally supported | Some specifications significant, others not; or confirmed in subset |
| **Suggestive** | Plausible but uncertain | Below significance threshold, small N, or wide confidence intervals |
| **Not confirmed** | Null or contradicted | p > 0.05 across specifications, or effect in opposite direction |

## Central Thesis: Three Tensions

This briefing reads the NZ polling dataset through five political theorists to surface three tensions that coexist without resolution. First, NZ's political volatility is downstream of **scarcity politics**: distributional coalitions block abundance, voters punish incumbents for material conditions governments struggle to change (Olson 1982; Klein & Thompson). Second, the mechanism of accountability is real but shallow: retrospective pocketbook voting, partisan perceptual screens, and myopic retrospection show voters reacting to felt experience rather than deliberating over policy — **democratic shallowness** in Arendt's sense of "the social" displacing "the political." Third, despite these pathologies, NZ's institutions absorb the resulting shocks without breaking — MMP channels discontent into coalition bargaining, alternation is orderly, and the system sits firmly in the **narrow corridor** of Acemoglu and Robinson (2019). The three tensions are genuine. This briefing holds them in place rather than collapsing them into a single narrative.

### Theorist Key

| Theorist | Core Idea | What NZ Evidence Tests |
|----------|-----------|----------------------|
| **Olson** (1965, 1982) | Distributional coalitions accumulate in stable democracies, producing institutional sclerosis | NZ as 170-year democracy: does coalition accumulation explain housing/productivity failure? |
| **Klein & Thompson** | Supply-side blockages and vetocracy prevent abundance despite resources | RMA, housing crisis, null house price finding |
| **Arendt** (1958) | "The social" — economic necessity — displaces genuine political action | Does the causal chain (costs -> salience -> approval -> vote) reduce politics to household management? |
| **Fukuyama** (2014, 2018) | State capacity, political decay, identity politics | State capacity paradox: top-tier governance, bottom-third productivity. Maori identity as cleavage. |
| **Acemoglu & Robinson** (2012, 2019) | Inclusive vs extractive institutions; the narrow corridor | Does NZ sit in the corridor? Can inclusive institutions produce exclusionary outcomes? |

## 1. The Scarcity Trap

*Primary: Olson (1965, 1982), Klein & Thompson. Secondary: Fukuyama (2014)*

The central economic finding of this project is a causal chain: material costs drive issue salience, salience drives government approval, approval drives vote intention (see *Economic Briefing*, Section 1). Mortgage rate rises are 68% mediated through approval (Sobel z = -3.32, p = 0.001). Petrol price rises are 49% mediated (Sobel z = -3.11, p = 0.002). Cost-of-living salience alone explains 74% of government approval variance [Confirmed]. Both mortgage rates (Granger F = 13.8, p = 0.0003) and petrol prices (Granger F = 12.0, p = 0.0006) are confirmed causal [Confirmed]. This chain describes a polity in which material scarcity — not ideology, not leadership, not foreign policy — is the master driver of political dynamics. The question is why the scarcity persists.

Olson's *Rise and Decline of Nations* (1982) argues that stable democracies accumulate distributional coalitions — industry groups, professional associations, regulatory bodies — that individually capture small rents but collectively produce institutional sclerosis. NZ is a 170-year continuous democracy (Freedom House: consistently Free, PR = 1, CL = 1 since 1972). On Olson's theory, it should exhibit maximum sclerosis. The comparative data are consistent with this prediction. NZ labour productivity is $55.4/hour (2023), just 74.8% of the OECD average, ranked 28th of 37 OECD countries. The price-to-income ratio peaked at 136.1 in 2021 and remains at 109.6 (2024), ranked 19th of 33 OECD countries. These are not the indicators of a resource-poor country — NZ has land, energy, and a small population. They are the indicators of a country that cannot convert resources into output because the permission to build is blocked.

Klein and Thompson's abundance agenda provides the mechanism Olson's theory implies. NZ's Resource Management Act (RMA, 1991) created a vetocracy in land use: any interested party could object to development, producing decades of underbuilding in housing and infrastructure. The result is visible in the data. House prices show no significant relationship with incumbent polling (r = -0.069, p = 0.427) [Not confirmed] — the null finding that is, from a theoretical standpoint, the most revealing result in the dataset. The wealth effect (homeowners benefit from rising prices) and the affordability grievance (renters and first-home buyers are locked out) cancel each other at the aggregate level. This is the Olson equilibrium in data: the distributional coalition of existing homeowners benefits from scarcity, the excluded bear the cost, and the aggregate political signal is zero. Neither party has an electoral incentive to break the equilibrium because the winners and losers are roughly equal in number.

The state capacity paradox deepens the puzzle. NZ ranks 1st-12th across all six World Bank Worldwide Governance Indicators dimensions (2023): 1st for Political Stability, 2nd for Voice and Accountability, 3rd for Regulatory Quality, 4th for Control of Corruption, 8th for Rule of Law, 12th for Government Effectiveness. The mean WGI score has been stable at 1.69-1.84 since 1996. On Fukuyama's framework — where state capacity is the prerequisite for effective governance — NZ should be an exemplary performer. It is, on process. It is not, on outcomes. This is a form of what Fukuyama (2014) calls "political decay" — not the decay of state capacity itself, but the capture of high-capacity institutions by distributional interests that redirect state power from public goods to private rents.

The regime-dependence finding illustrates how scarcity traps both sides of politics. Under Labour (2017-2023), rising mortgage rates signalled cost-of-living pain and correlated with falling approval (r = -0.814). Under National (2023-present), falling mortgage rates signal RBNZ emergency response to recession and correlate with falling approval (r = +0.829) [Confirmed]. The same indicator punishes both governments because neither can resolve the underlying scarcity. Issue ownership compounds the trap: cost-of-living salience activates the National brand (Lab r = -0.885), but when National governs, it faces the same cost pressures that destroyed Labour's support. The electorate cycles between parties without either delivering abundance. This is Olson's prediction: democratic alternation without structural reform.

## 2. The Accountability Paradox

*Primary: Arendt (1958), Fiorina (1981). Secondary: Achen & Bartels (2016)*

NZ voters hold governments accountable. Retrospective economic voting is confirmed in all 10 NZES elections (1990-2023), with a mean |r| = 0.376, all p < 0.001 [Confirmed]. Prospective voting loses 4 out of 5 head-to-head tests against retrospective (mean |r| retro = 0.376 vs prospective = 0.222) [Confirmed]. Sociotropic perception dominates egotropic (r = 0.361 vs 0.153) — voters respond to national conditions, not just personal finances. Government approval is the master variable: R squared = 0.675 alone, each +1pt on the Ipsos 0-10 scale translates to approximately +5.7pp incumbent vote [Confirmed]. The mechanism works. The question is whether it constitutes genuine political judgement.

Arendt's distinction in *The Human Condition* (1958) between "the political" — collective action on matters of shared concern — and "the social" — the management of economic necessity — provides a framework for reading the causal chain. The chain runs: mortgage rates -> cost-of-living salience -> government approval -> vote intention. Every link is economic. The master issue variable is cost-of-living salience (74% of approval variance). Two issue salience variables (cost-of-living plus unemployment) explain 88% of approval. In Arendt's terms, NZ electoral politics has been substantially colonised by "the social." Voters are not deliberating over competing visions of the good society; they are reacting to the price of groceries and the mortgage refix rate. This is labour, not action.

Three findings deepen the concern. First, the partisan perceptual screen: government supporters rate the economy +0.68 scale points more positively than opposition supporters (on a 5-point scale), every year, all p < 0.001 [Confirmed]. The gap is largest in late-term governments (+1.00-1.01 in 2014 and 2017) and smallest during crises when reality is unambiguous (+0.30-0.34 in 2008 and 2020). This means accountability is partly circular — voters' perceptions of the economy are shaped by their prior vote intention, which then shapes their next vote intention. The causal arrow runs both ways, and the perceptual screen is the deepest challenge to any rationalist account of democratic accountability (see *Political Briefing*, Section 2).

Second, the myopic retrospection finding [Suggestive]: election-year GDP growth correlates with incumbent fate (r = +0.588, p = 0.096) while early-term growth shows no relationship (r = -0.114, p = 0.770). This is directionally consistent with Achen and Bartels' (2016) thesis that voters are "blind retrospectors" who evaluate governments based on recent experience rather than full-term performance. The evidence is below conventional significance (N = 9, confidence interval approximately -0.10 to +0.90), but the pattern — late-term matters, early-term does not — is what the theory predicts. If confirmed, it means voters are not even competent retrospective evaluators: they reward or punish based on the last year, not the full record (see *Economic Briefing*, Section 9).

Third, polling follows a random walk: no indicator significantly predicts quarter-to-quarter vote changes (all p > 0.2), and the naive baseline (this quarter equals next quarter) beats all model-based forecasts with MAE = 2.64pp [Confirmed]. This is consistent with a polity where votes respond to felt experience in real time but cannot be anticipated because future economic shocks are unpredictable. Voters are reactive, not deliberative.

Against this reading, two findings suggest residual political judgement. Split-ticket voting under MMP runs at approximately 30%, with minor party voters lending electorate votes to major parties approximately 50% of the time [Partial]. This is strategic behaviour — voters understand the dual-vote system and exploit it rationally. And left-right positional voting is not only strong (mean r = 0.698) but strengthening over time (r = 0.533 in 1990 to r = 0.760 in 2023) without the L-R distribution polarising [Confirmed]. Voters are sorting ideologically — increasingly voting in line with their stated left-right position — even as the centre holds. This is not the behaviour of pure pocketbook reactors. It suggests that ideological commitments structure the baseline vote, and pocketbook effects perturb it. Arendt's colonisation of the political by the social is real but incomplete. NZ trust in government stands at 46% (vs OECD 39%), even as approval sits at a record low 3.9/10 — voters distinguish between the system (which they trust) and the current government (which they do not).

## 3. Identity, Realignment, and the New Cleavage

*Primary: Fukuyama (2018). Secondary: Ford & Jennings (2020)*

Dalton's (2000) dealignment thesis predicts that class, religion, and demographic cleavages weaken as education rises and partisan attachments loosen. The NZ evidence does not confirm this: the full demographic model (age + gender + education + Maori identity) produces pseudo-R squared values of 0.032 in 1996 rising to 0.044 in 2023 (trend r = +0.532, p = 0.114) [Not confirmed]. Total demographic predictive power is stable to slightly increasing, not declining.

But the composition has shifted dramatically. The age effect reversed: young voters leaned National in the 1990s but now lean Labour, producing a 20pp-plus gap with older voters favouring National since 2005. Gender and education effects are weakening toward zero — partial dealignment on these traditional cleavages. The dominant change is the strengthening of Maori identity as the primary demographic predictor of vote choice. Maori voters are 61-86% less likely to vote National (OR = 0.14-0.39), and this effect has strengthened since 1996, becoming the single most powerful demographic predictor by 2011 [Confirmed] (see *Political Briefing*, Section 5).

Fukuyama's *Identity* (2018) argues that identity politics — the demand for recognition of group dignity — is the defining dynamic of 21st-century democracy, typically entering as a destabilising force through populist movements. NZ is a partial counter-case. Maori identity has entered politics through institutional channels: Te Pati Maori, the Maori electoral roll, dedicated Maori seats, and co-governance arrangements (such as Three Waters). The identity cleavage is strengthening, but it is being processed through the existing institutional framework rather than erupting as anti-system populism. This is consistent with Ford and Jennings' (2020) realignment thesis: old cleavages (class, gender) weaken while new cleavages (ethnic identity) emerge, but the overall predictive power of demographics remains stable because one replaces the other.

The L-R positional voting finding adds nuance. Ideological self-placement predicts the National-vs-Labour vote with mean r = 0.698, and this has strengthened from 0.533 (1990) to 0.760 (2023) without the left-right distribution polarising — the mean self-placement remains at approximately 5.0 (centre) [Confirmed]. Voters are ideologically sorting — voting more consistently with their stated position — without moving to the extremes. This is realignment without polarisation, a combination that Fukuyama's framework does not anticipate (he expects identity politics to produce affective polarisation). NZ may be benefiting from MMP's capacity to absorb identity demands through minor parties and coalition bargaining rather than forcing them into a two-party zero-sum contest.

The reduced model (age + gender only, excluding Maori identity) does show dealignment: pseudo-R squared dropped 47% from 0.011 in 1996 to 0.006 in 2023. The old class-linked cleavages are fading. They are being replaced, not by nothing, but by ethnic identity as the dominant demographic structuring variable. NZ is experiencing realignment in Ford and Jennings' sense: a rotation of the cleavage axis, not a dissolution of cleavage politics.

## 4. The Narrow Corridor: Why NZ Does Not Break

*Primary: Acemoglu & Robinson (2019). Secondary: Fukuyama (2014)*

Acemoglu and Robinson's *Narrow Corridor* (2019) argues that liberty and prosperity require a balance between state capacity and societal mobilisation — the "corridor" where the state is strong enough to enforce rules but not so strong that it escapes accountability. NZ sits firmly and deeply within this corridor, and the polling data show why.

The institutional evidence is unambiguous. Freedom House rates NZ as consistently Free with PR = 1, CL = 1 in every year since 1972 — 52 consecutive years. WGI scores place NZ 1st-12th across all six governance dimensions (2023), with the mean score stable at 1.69-1.84 since 1996. Trust in government (46%) exceeds the OECD average (39%). These are the structural indicators of a wide corridor — high state capacity and high societal capacity coexisting.

The polling mechanics confirm that the corridor functions. MMP produces no third-party squeeze (p = 0.682) [Not confirmed for squeeze], split-ticket voting at approximately 30%, and sustained minor party viability (18.6% combined mean support) [Partial]. Discontent flows to the opposition major party, not to anti-system movements: the zero-sum bloc dynamic (National-Labour r = -0.310) channels economic frustration into orderly alternation rather than institutional challenge [Confirmed]. Polls converge toward the result as election day approaches (National: r = 0.243, Labour: r = 0.359, both p < 0.001) [Confirmed], and the winner is called correctly 9 out of 10 elections. Incumbent decay is universal (-0.114 pp/month) but moderate, and mean reversion operates reliably (67% of extreme polls revert). The system self-corrects.

NZ First serves as a case study in corridor mechanics. When immigration salience rises and NZ First is in opposition, it gains support (8.0% mean at 71k/yr migration in 2015-17). When NZ First enters government, its support collapses regardless of migration levels (3.1% in 2018-20 at 52k/yr) [Partial]. The system absorbs populist energy by co-opting it into government: the protest party enters the corridor and is domesticated by the responsibilities of coalition. This is precisely the dynamic Acemoglu and Robinson describe — society mobilises, the state responds by incorporating the challenge, and the corridor holds.

But the corridor has limits. The scarcity trap (Section 1) shows that inclusive institutions do not guarantee inclusive outcomes. NZ's governance indicators are world-class; its productivity and housing outcomes are not. The narrow corridor keeps the system stable, but stability is not the same as prosperity. The corridor contains the political consequences of scarcity — there is no breakdown, no authoritarian turn, no anti-system movement gaining traction — but it does not resolve the scarcity itself. Voters cycle between parties (see Section 2), each government is punished for conditions it cannot change (see Section 1), and the corridor absorbs the resulting frustration without transmitting it into structural reform.

## 5. Distributional Coalitions and the Abundance Failure

*Primary: Olson (1982). Secondary: Klein & Thompson, Acemoglu & Robinson*

The house price null finding (r = -0.069, p = 0.427) [Not confirmed] is the single most theoretically significant result in the dataset. It means that rising house prices have no net effect on incumbent polling. The reason, as argued in Section 1, is that homeowners and excluded buyers cancel each other out. But this cancellation is not neutral — it is the mechanism by which scarcity persists. When the political costs of high prices (angry renters) equal the political costs of falling prices (angry homeowners), neither party has an electoral incentive to increase supply. The null finding is the Olson equilibrium made visible: the distributional coalition of property owners blocks abundance, and the political system registers no net signal to change course.

The rental inflation finding reinforces this interpretation. Rental CPI year-on-year change shows a strong level correlation with incumbent vote share (r = -0.731) but fails Granger causality (all lags p > 0.27) and loses significance under Newey-West HAC (OLS p = 0.001 to NW p = 0.112) [Associational only]. Renters experience scarcity acutely — rental inflation tracks incumbent unpopularity — but they lack the political voice to produce a causal effect on polling. Rental and mortgage inflation are uncorrelated (r = 0.052), confirming they represent different channels affecting different demographics. But only mortgage rates (affecting the median voter who owns property) survive as a confirmed causal indicator. Renters' pain is real but politically ineffective.

The minor party insulation finding completes the picture. Mortgage rate-rise episodes (2021-2023) produced National +3.4pp, Labour -3.8pp, but Green +0.1pp, NZ First +0.1pp, ACT -0.2pp [Confirmed]. Economic voting is a major-party phenomenon. Green voters (who skew young and renting) and ACT voters (who skew older and property-owning) do not swing on pocketbook costs. This means the parties most likely to advocate for structural reform — the Greens on housing supply, ACT on deregulation — draw their support from bases that are insulated from the very pocketbook pressures that drive the electoral cycle. The voters who swing on costs vote for major parties that alternate in office without resolving the underlying scarcity.

NZ's productivity position reinforces the Olson diagnosis. At $55.4/hour (2023), NZ is 74.8% of the OECD average, ranked 28th of 37 countries. The price-to-income ratio peaked at 136.1 in 2021. These are the outcomes of a high-governance, low-productivity economy — exactly what Olson predicts for a long-stable democracy where distributional coalitions have had generations to accumulate. Klein and Thompson would identify the specific mechanism: NZ's planning regime (the RMA and its successors) functions as a vetocracy that blocks the supply-side response. Acemoglu and Robinson's framework adds a further dimension: NZ's housing market may constitute an extractive pocket within an otherwise inclusive institutional corridor. The institutions are inclusive in process (rule of law, accountability, low corruption) but produce extractive outcomes in the specific domain of land use and housing, where incumbent property owners capture regulatory rents.

## 6. Propositions and Open Questions

The three-tensions framework generates several testable propositions for future analysis and for interpreting the 2026 election and beyond.

**P1 (Scarcity persistence):** If the Olson/Klein thesis is correct, housing and infrastructure reform will be blocked regardless of which party governs. The prediction is that the house price null finding (r = -0.069) will persist in future elections, and that whichever party attempts supply-side reform will face distributional coalition resistance that either defeats the reform or costs the party support. The Resource Management Act replacement (2023-2024) is the critical test case.

**P2 (Accountability quality):** If the myopic retrospection finding strengthens with additional elections (currently r = +0.588, p = 0.096, N = 9), it would confirm that NZ voters are "blind retrospectors" in Achen and Bartels' sense — responsive to recent conditions, not cumulative performance. The 2026 election is a test: if the economy recovers substantially in 2025-2026 but voters still punish National for the 2024 recession, myopic retrospection would be disconfirmed. If late-2026 conditions dominate early-term performance in voters' minds, it would be further supported.

**P3 (Identity trajectory):** Fukuyama predicts that identity politics produces polarisation. NZ's evidence so far — ideological sorting without distribution polarisation, Maori identity channelled through institutional mechanisms — contradicts this. The proposition is that Te Pati Maori's electoral trajectory and the co-governance debate will test whether identity demands can continue to be processed through the corridor or whether they generate the polarisation Fukuyama expects. A rising Maori identity cleavage (OR strengthening beyond current 0.14-0.39 range) combined with stable L-R distribution would confirm the NZ counter-case.

**P4 (Corridor resilience):** The narrow corridor prediction is that NZ First will continue to function as a safety valve — gaining support when populist energy rises, losing it when incorporated into government. The 2026 election tests this: NZ First is polling approximately 10% while in government, historically unusual. If this reflects Peters' personal brand rather than genuine anti-system mobilisation, NZ First should decline toward election day as voters face the coalition-vs-opposition binary.

**P5 (Approval convergence):** The current gap between government approval (3.9/10, implying approximately 22% incumbent vote at historical conversion rates) and actual National polling (approximately 32%) is the largest in the dataset. Either approval will recover or polling will decline further. The proposition is that the gap will close primarily through polling decline, because approval recovery requires either economic improvement that voters credit to the government (difficult given the perceptual screen) or a salience shift away from cost-of-living (which requires an exogenous shock). See *Economic Briefing*, Section 11 for current indicator trajectories.

**P6 (Renter political voice):** The rental inflation finding (r = -0.731 levels, but fails Granger and Newey-West) suggests renters lack causal political influence despite experiencing acute scarcity. If renter demographics shift — through rising rental populations or political mobilisation — this null Granger finding should eventually flip to significance. The proportion of renters vs owners is the key structural variable to track.

### Evidence Gaps

The three-tensions framework exposes several gaps in the current evidence base. First, there is no direct measure of distributional coalition density in NZ — the Olson thesis is inferred from outcomes (productivity, housing) rather than measured from inputs (number of blocking coalitions, regulatory capture incidents). Second, the myopic retrospection finding rests on N = 9 elections with wide confidence intervals; it will take several more electoral cycles to confirm or disconfirm. Third, the Arendt critique of democratic shallowness is fundamentally a normative claim that the data can illustrate but not resolve — whether pocketbook voting constitutes "adequate" accountability depends on prior commitments about what democracy is for. Fourth, the narrow corridor framework lacks a clear failure condition for NZ: what would it look like for NZ to exit the corridor? The data show a system that absorbs shocks, but the theory needs to specify what shock would be too large to absorb. Fifth, the comparative data (WGI, OECD productivity, price-to-income) provide cross-sectional positioning but not the panel variation needed to test whether NZ's governance-productivity gap is widening or narrowing relative to peers.
