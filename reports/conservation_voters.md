# Conservation Voters in New Zealand: A Non-Left Environmentalist Cohort

**Analysis of NZES data 1993–2023**

---

## The Theory

The standard account of environmental voting in New Zealand treats it as a left-wing phenomenon: environmentally-concerned voters go to the Green Party, and to a lesser extent Labour. But this may miss a second, distinct group.

New Zealand has a large population of people whose relationship to nature is recreational and instrumental rather than ideological — trampers, hunters, anglers, farmers with conservation values. These people care about clean rivers for fishing, public access to conservation land, pest control policy, and the health of game species. Their conservation values don't naturally translate into the broader Green platform (climate policy, social progressivism, anti-growth economics). They're not locked into the left.

The theory, then: there is a meaningful cohort of environmentally-concerned voters who are nonetheless **available to National**, and who can be activated by specific conservation causes — water quality, 1080, marine reserves, hunting access — in ways that conventional economic-issues analysis would miss.

---

## Data and Method

The New Zealand Election Study (NZES) surveys voters after each general election, covering 1990–2023 with approximately 1,000–4,000 respondents per wave. The key variables are:

- **Environment/economy trade-off**: a 1–7 scale where 1 = "do more to protect the environment even at the cost of economic development" and 7 = "do more to encourage economic development even at some cost to the environment." Available in all years from 1993 onward.
- **Party vote**: self-reported party vote cast in the election.
- **"Would never vote for" each party**: available 2008–2023, asking which parties the respondent would categorically rule out.
- **Environmental group membership**: available 1996–2005.
- **Prior vote**: what party the respondent voted for in the previous election, available 2014–2020.

**Operationalising the theory:**

- *Pro-environment voters*: those scoring 1–3 on the 1–7 scale (the environmental half of the distribution).
- *Ideological environmentalists*: pro-environment voters who would never vote National.
- *Available conservationists*: pro-environment voters who would never vote Green — i.e., people with environmental concerns who have nonetheless ruled out the party most identified with environmentalism.

---

## Finding 1: National Has Always Had a Share of Pro-Environment Voters

Among voters who score on the pro-environment side of the issues scale, National consistently attracts a meaningful vote share — not a rounding error, but a substantial fraction.

| Year | National | Labour | Green | NZ First | N (pro-env) |
|------|----------|--------|-------|----------|-------------|
| 1993 | 33.3% | 31.5% | — | 9.6% | 615 |
| 1996 | 25.4% | 30.9% | — | 14.1% | 1,470 |
| 1999 | 21.6% | 38.7% | 5.5% | 4.0% | 758 |
| 2002 | 14.5% | 43.4% | 15.2% | 8.1% | 1,612 |
| 2005 | 20.9% | 45.9% | 9.9% | 5.9% | 1,633 |
| 2008 | 22.6% | 41.5% | 15.5% | 5.3% | 857 |
| 2014 | 27.6% | 29.2% | 22.8% | 9.2% | 1,270 |
| 2017 | 29.1% | 43.1% | 12.2% | 6.9% | 1,999 |
| 2020 | 11.4% | 57.9% | 16.7% | 2.4% | 1,969 |
| 2023 | 17.2% | 34.4% | 25.3% | 4.1% | 1,020 |

*Green not available as a separate party in 1993 and 1996.*

Several things stand out. First, National's share of pro-environment voters is never negligible — it ranges from 11% (2020, the COVID landslide) to 33% (1993, before MMP and before the Greens existed as a standalone party). Second, the Greens do not monopolise environmentally-concerned voters even once they're a major party; Labour consistently outperforms them among this group. Third, the variation in National's share — high in ordinary elections, low in landslide Labour years — is exactly the pattern of a genuine swing cohort rather than a locked-in partisan one.

---

## Finding 2: The "Available Conservationist" Segment Is Real, Stable, and Swing

The cleaner test is to identify voters who are simultaneously pro-environment *and* would categorically never vote Green. This rules out people who simply haven't thought about the issue — anyone in this group has both environmental concerns and a positive rejection of the main environmental party.

This "available conservationist" group is consistently **10–16% of all pro-environment voters** across every election from 2008 to 2023.

| Year | Never-Green pro-env voters (% of all pro-env) | Their vote: National | Labour | NZ First |
|------|------------------------------------------------|----------------------|--------|----------|
| 2008 | 10.5% (n=90) | 52.2% | 18.9% | 10.0% |
| 2014 | 12.3% (n=156) | 55.8% | 16.0% | 10.3% |
| 2017 | 15.1% (n=301) | 65.1% | 15.3% | 12.6% |
| 2020 | 16.3% (n=320) | 34.1% | 30.6% | 5.6% |
| 2023 | 13.6% (n=139) | 56.8% | 12.2% | 10.1% |

**In normal election years, roughly 55–65% of this group votes National.** The remainder splits mainly between Labour and NZ First, with very small shares going elsewhere.

The 2020 result is the most revealing. In that extraordinary election — with Ardern at peak popularity, a COVID response widely judged successful, and an opposition in disarray — this cohort's National vote collapsed from 65% to 34%, and Labour absorbed most of the switch (rising to 31%). This is textbook swing voter behaviour. These are not soft partisans who sometimes vote wrong; they are genuine floaters whose vote is determined by the political environment rather than party loyalty.

In contrast, the ideological environmentalist group (pro-environment voters who would never vote National) is larger (31–39% of pro-env voters in 2014–2020) but by definition not available to National at all.

---

## Finding 3: Environmental Group Members Aren't All on the Left

Between 1996 and 1999, approximately **19% of environmental group members voted National** — compared with 31% of non-members in 1996. This confirms that even people committed enough to join an environmental organisation are not uniformly left-wing voters. (The 2002 and 2005 data has coding anomalies that make those years unreliable for this analysis.)

The implication is that the "available conservationist" cohort includes people with genuine, active conservation commitments, not just vague pro-environment sentiments.

---

## Finding 4: The Natural Experiments Were Too Small to Profile

In 2014, the Ban1080 Party contested the election on a platform explicitly targeting hunters and farmers opposed to aerial poison drops — a single-issue conservation vehicle with no broader left-wing agenda. In 2020, the Outdoor Recreation Party made a similar pitch.

Both parties were too small to profile statistically from NZES samples (Ban1080 received 0.03% of the party vote; Outdoor Recreation received a comparable share). However, the *existence* of these parties is itself informative. There is sufficient demand for a non-left conservation vehicle that people repeatedly attempt to build one. The NZES also records that Ban1080 appeared as a coded option in both 2014 and 2017 surveys, and that some respondents placed it on their "would never vote for" lists — suggesting a degree of awareness disproportionate to its vote share.

---

## How Large Is This Cohort?

Roughly 40–50% of New Zealanders score on the pro-environment side of the 1–7 issues scale in any given election year. Of those, approximately 12–16% are "available conservationists" by our definition. That works out to roughly **5–8% of the total electorate** — several times the threshold required to swing a close election.

National's baseline vote share among this group in normal years is 55–65%. In a good Labour year (2020), that drops to 34%. The swing potential — the difference between a good and bad year for National among this cohort — is roughly 20–30 percentage points on a group that's 5–8% of the electorate, or 1–2 percentage points of the total party vote. Non-trivial in a close election.

---

## What Would Activate This Cohort?

The NZES does not directly measure sensitivity to specific conservation issues, so we can't directly test whether 1080 controversy, marine reserve decisions, or freshwater policy moves this group independently of other political factors. But the structure of the data supports the activation theory:

1. The cohort is large enough to matter in close elections.
2. Their vote swings dramatically with political context — they're not habitual National voters.
3. Their environmental concern is genuine: they score 1–3 on the issues scale while still refusing to vote Green.
4. The repeated emergence of single-issue conservation parties (Ban1080, Outdoor Recreation) suggests they're aware of being politically homeless on this issue.

The most plausible reading is that National could do meaningfully better with this group by making credible commitments on specific conservation issues that speak to recreational users — water quality in fishable rivers, hunting access on public land, responsible pest management — rather than climate and emissions, which the Green Party has claimed as its signature territory.

---

## Caveats

**The identity of the cohort is inferred, not observed.** We cannot directly identify hunters, fishers, or trampers in NZES data. The "available conservationist" segment is defined behaviourally (pro-environment + never-Green) rather than demographically. It likely includes people with quite different motivations — rural landowners, farmers with conservation values, urban nature enthusiasts who distrust the Greens' broader politics.

**"Never-vote-Green" may be driven by non-conservation reasons.** Some people in this segment may reject the Greens on social, economic, or identity grounds unrelated to conservation. Their environmental concern may be incidental to their vote decision.

**The effect of specific conservation events is untested.** We've established the cohort exists and swings; we haven't shown that conservation policy announcements specifically move them. Doing that would require either a panel study tracking voters around specific conservation decisions, or analysis of National polling in high-hunting/fishing electorates around major conservation controversies.

---

## Summary

| Claim | Evidence | Verdict |
|-------|----------|---------|
| A pro-environment cohort votes National | National gets 11–33% of pro-env voters across all elections | **Confirmed** |
| The cohort is available (not locked in) | Their National vote swings from 65% to 34% between elections | **Confirmed** |
| The cohort rejects the Greens specifically | 10–16% of pro-env voters consistently rule out Green | **Confirmed** |
| The cohort is large enough to matter | ~5–8% of total electorate | **Yes** |
| Conservation issues specifically activate them | Not directly testable from NZES | **Untested** |
| They're recreational users (hunters/fishers) | Not directly observable in NZES | **Inferred** |

The theory is well-supported in its testable elements. A non-left environmental cohort exists, is consistently available to National, and swings meaningfully with political conditions. Whether specific conservation policy is the mechanism — as opposed to general anti-Green sentiment or rural identity — remains an open question that would require different data to answer.
