# Media and Politics panel: draft answers

*Prepared 19 October 2026 for the panel on Wednesday 21 October; questions 1 and 2 revised 21 October*

Draft answers, each about 60–90 seconds spoken. These questions are mostly about judgement rather than data, so each answer pairs a position with the one or two pieces of evidence that support it. The evidence comes from this project (published polls, Ipsos Issues Monitor, NZ Election Study, OECD confidence indices), the 2026 JMAD *Trust in News* report, and the ministerial-diaries project. *Italic notes* mark where you need to add your own view or check a fact before using it. New numbers come from `reports/media_panel_numbers.py`.

## 1. How should government and political leaders work with business to improve policymaking?

**Listen to business, but ask which business is talking.** What's good for the economy and what's good for the firms already in a market are often different things. Business knows how a rule will work in practice, and government should use that knowledge. But the voice that reaches ministers usually belongs to an industry body, and industry bodies represent incumbents. An incumbent's interest is often in keeping competitors out. So when an industry body says a licensing rule, a consenting requirement or a subsidy would be "good for business", politicians should ask: good for which businesses, and who pays?

**This matters more in New Zealand because we have a competition problem.**

- **Business dynamism is falling.** The Commerce Commission's first *State of Competition* report (May 2026, firm-level data for 2001–23) found that business entry and exit rates have "fallen materially". New entrants gain less traction, and their market share five years after entry is about the same as after one year, so the ones that get in don't grow. "Bigger older businesses are becoming more entrenched."
- **The weakest competition is in the sectors everyone else buys from.** The least competitive industries were electricity, gas and water; finance and insurance; telecommunications and media; and mining. These are inputs to the rest of the economy, so their margins flow through into everyone else's costs.
- **The market studies keep finding the same thing.** Groceries (2022): excess profits of about $1 million a day, and supermarkets using land covenants to stop rivals opening nearby. Building supplies (2022): GIB has about 94% of the plasterboard market, partly because the consenting system rewards specifying the familiar product; the 2022 GIB shortage showed what that costs. The Commission has since taken Winstone Wallboards to court over its rebates. Banking (2024): a "stable oligopoly", with four Australian-owned banks holding about 85% of lending.

Several of those barriers come from regulation rather than geography: consenting rules, land covenants, product approvals. They were built or tolerated with industry input.

**What that means for how government works with business:**

- **Separate the advice from the decision.** Ask business how a policy will work, not whether it should exist. "Will this compliance regime cost you $5,000 or $500,000?" is expertise. "Please don't let anyone else in" is lobbying.
- **Hear from the firms that aren't in the market yet.** An industry body speaks for its members. The firm that would enter if a barrier came down isn't in the room, and may not exist yet. The Commerce Commission, overseas suppliers and consumer groups can speak for that missing competitor. *Check before using: the Productivity Commission, which did that job at arm's length, was disestablished in 2024.*
- **Treat corporate welfare as spending, not economic strategy.** Paying to keep a particular firm going (the Key government's $30 million to Rio Tinto for Tiwai Point in 2013, for example) is a transfer from taxpayers to shareholders, and it should face the same scrutiny as any other spending.
- **Publish who was consulted and what they said.**

**The data adds a warning: business sentiment isn't the same as the economy.** The OECD's business confidence index for New Zealand fell in the six months after Labour took office in 1999 (−2.1 points, about one standard deviation) and in 2017 (−1.3). It barely moved in 2008 and rose slightly in 2023. It predicts next quarter's GDP growth no better than consumer confidence does (r=0.26 for both). Business confidence is one interest group's mood, partly about who's in government. It's not a neutral reading of the economy.

*Possible examples: one where a barrier came down despite incumbent objections (check before using: the 2025 law letting building products certified in trusted overseas jurisdictions skip separate NZ approval) and one where lobbying won (the 2024 smokefree repeal).*

## 2. What role do media and political leaders play in shaping public understanding of the economy?

**Most of what politicians say about the economy is marketing, and part of the media's job is to counteract it.** Governments take credit for things they didn't cause. Oppositions blame them for things they couldn't control. Both sides pick the numbers that sell their story. Journalism should tell people what's actually happening.

**The marketing works, but mostly on people who already agree.** In the NZ Election Study, government and opposition voters looking at the same economy rate it about two-thirds of a point apart on the survey scale, in every election since 1990. Partisans hear economic messages as signals from their team, not as information. Independent explanation matters most for the voters who aren't committed.

**Three things media can do that politicians won't:**

**1. Tell people what a number means.** I once had a press gallery journalist ask me whether 7% inflation was a good or bad thing. It's a fair question, because most people have no benchmark. Annual inflation peaked at 7.3% in the June 2022 quarter, the highest since 1990. The Reserve Bank's target is 1–3%. At 7%, prices double in about ten years; at 2%, in about 35. The public had noticed before most of the commentary caught up. Ipsos cost-of-living concern went from 31% in October 2021 to 53% in February 2022, while inflation went from 4.9% to 6.9%.

The reverse problem is happening now. Annual inflation was 3.1% at the end of 2025, so "inflation is under control" is true. But prices are 24% higher than in early 2021, and food is 28% higher. People judge the price level, not the rate: since 2022, cost-of-living concern has tracked food inflation (r=0.73) more closely than headline inflation (r=0.47), and petrol prices move government support within the month. Official statistics like GDP and unemployment barely move votes, because people experience prices, not "the economy". Explaining both the rate and the level is the job.

**2. Explain which story a number belongs to.** Under Labour, rising mortgage rates went with falling government approval. Under National, *falling* rates went with falling approval, because the cuts meant the Reserve Bank was responding to a recession. Politicians will claim whichever reading suits them, so it falls to journalists to explain which one is true.

**3. Bring new ideas into the conversation, and test them.** I wrote a lot about the abundance agenda (Klein and Thompson's argument that the problem for the left isn't too little ambition but a state that can't build) when it was salient in centre-left politics. It was worth covering because it's a critique of the left's own record, and it connects to the first question: many of the barriers to building are protections for incumbents. But I'm still sceptical that a left-wing coalition would get the state to deliver everything it promises. The record is a warning:

- **KiwiBuild** promised 100,000 homes over ten years. By mid-2019 it had finished 258 against a first-year target of 1,000, and the target was dropped that September.
- **Auckland light rail** ran for six years and spent $228 million (the government's figure; Labour disputes it) without starting construction. It was cancelled in January 2024.

The question to put to any party promising delivery is what they'll change about the machinery so it happens this time. The right has its own version: National campaigned in 2023 as the party that would fix the economy, and business confidence rose when it won. But GDP fell for two quarters in mid-2024 (−1.7% combined).

## 3. What's driving the improvement in trust in news?

**The facts:** the JMAD report (Horizon Research, 1,040 adults, February 2026) found:

| Measure | 2025 | 2026 |
|---|---:|---:|
| Trust most news most of the time | 32% | **37%** |
| Trust the news I use | 45% | 50% |
| Trust news on social media | 13% | 17% |
| Actively avoid news at least sometimes | 73% | 78% |

That's the first rise after six years of decline, but trust is still well below its 2020 peak (53%) and the international average (40%). A separate Acumen survey found a similar rise (35% → 39%), so it's probably real.

**Three likely drivers:**

1. **A flood of AI content and influencers makes journalism worth more.** The report's authors argue people are using news media to verify what they see online. Around 60% are uncomfortable with news produced mainly by AI. Online slop makes a verified source more valuable.
2. **The Covid-era backlash is fading.** Trust peaked in 2020 during the pandemic rally and fell as that period turned bitter. Mistrust is partisan. It's lowest among 2023 voters for ACT, NZ First and Democracy NZ; 39% of National voters distrust the news, against 45–50% of Labour, Green and TOP voters who trust it. With a centre-right government in power for three years, the "media are close to the government" grievance has had less to feed on.
3. **Some of it may be a rebound.** 2025 was the lowest reading in the series. In our polling data, about two-thirds of extreme readings move back toward the average next time. One year's rise after a record low shouldn't be read as a trend yet.

**One warning sign:** trust and avoidance are rising together. More people trust the news, and more people are switching it off. The most common reasons for avoiding it are that it hurts their mood (53%) and that they're worn out (34%), not that they distrust it.

*Your own view: whether the closure of Newshub and the cuts elsewhere made people value what's left.*

## 4. Are public concerns about vested interests in policymaking well-founded?

**Yes, and our clean reputation is part of the reason they go unaddressed.** New Zealand placed 4th on Transparency International's 2025 Corruption Perceptions Index. But that index measures *perceived* corruption, mostly bribery and graft, as judged by experts and business people. It can't see what economists call "legal corruption": influence bought through channels the law allows (Kaufmann and Vicente 2011). We look clean partly because practices that are restricted or illegal across much of the OECD are legal here, so they never count as corruption.

**Legal in New Zealand, restricted in many comparable countries:**

| Practice | New Zealand | Elsewhere |
|---|---|---|
| Size of political donations | No limit, including from companies and trusts | Canada bans corporate and union donations and caps individual donations at a few thousand dollars. France bans corporate donations. NSW bans donations from property developers, tobacco, liquor and gambling interests. |
| Anonymous donations | Up to $61,425 per donor, $409,500 per party per cycle, through the Electoral Commission's "protected" route | *I haven't verified a country comparison for this one; use it as a New Zealand fact only* |
| Ex-minister becoming a lobbyist | Can start the next day | Stand-down periods in Canada, Ireland and Australia |
| Lobbying | No register, no code of conduct; the OECD's 2026 integrity outlook reports no system for registering or disclosing it | Registers in Ireland, Canada, Australia, the UK and the US |
| Ministerial conflicts of interest | Cabinet Manual convention, enforced by the PM | Statutory in many jurisdictions |
| Anti-corruption body | None. The Serious Fraud Office prosecutes, but nobody investigates misconduct short of crime | Australia set up a national anti-corruption commission in 2023; NSW has ICAC |

*Check before using: whether trust-held and spouse-held property is still left off MPs' register of pecuniary interests (reported as a loophole in 2008 and 2013).*

**The trend is going the wrong way.** New Zealand's score on the index has fallen from 91 in 2015 to 81 in 2025. Transparency International links the decline to a lack of anti-corruption legislation. The Electoral Amendment Act 2025 lengthened the deadline for reporting large donations from 10 to 20 working days.

Further evidence:

- **There are recent cases on both sides.** Labour's Stuart Nash was sacked in 2023 after emailing confidential Cabinet information to donors. The 2024 repeal of the smokefree generation law was a policy win for a heavily lobbying industry. The Fast-track Approvals Act gave ministers a direct role in choosing projects. *Check before using: reporting that some fast-track applicants had donated to coalition parties.*
- **Access shifts with government, in ways the public can only partly see.** In ministerial diaries, Federated Farmers appeared in 11 entries in the first half of 2023 and 32 in the first half of 2024, across 12 ministers rather than 8. That's disclosed diary entries, not meetings or influence, and diary practice varies by minister. But it's the kind of shift people suspect and usually can't see.

**Vested interests also shape the information environment, not just policy.** In the 2017–20 term, 70% of the published party-vote polls listed on Wikipedia were commissioned by media outlets. This term it's 27%. The Taxpayers' Union–Curia poll is now the most frequent public poll (35 this term, against 19 for 1News–Verian). To be fair, its numbers aren't an outlier: against other polls in the same month, it has the right bloc only about 0.7 points higher, and Roy Morgan differs from the pack by far more. But a lobby group now decides when one of the country's main polls goes out and which questions it publishes.

**On the other side:** "vested interests" isn't only business. Unions, health NGOs, iwi and environmental groups all lobby, and many of the loudest calls for lobbying reform come from advocacy groups with their own policy goals.

## 5. What measures would increase transparency on lobbying? Government- or industry-led?

**Government-led, light-touch, and set in law, with industry helping design it.** A voluntary industry code binds the firms that sign it, and the firms people worry about won't sign. The Ministry of Justice consulted on a voluntary code in 2024, and it went nowhere.

What would help, roughly in order of cost:

1. **Standardised, machine-readable ministerial diaries.** We already publish diaries; they just aren't usable. About 197,000 entries have been released since late 2017, but practice varies widely. Some ministers disclose 14 entries per month, others more than 100. One former minister's diary has entries in only 5 of 40 months. A common format, published monthly, naming organisations and topics, would cost little and close most of the gap. *Check before using: the diary figures are from the ministerial-diaries project's draft disclosure analysis, not yet reviewed.*
2. **A lobbying register** covering paid lobbyists and in-house government-relations staff: who they represent, which minister or agency they met, and on what topic. Ireland's 2015 regime is a good model.
3. **A stand-down period** of 12–18 months before former ministers and senior political staff can lobby their former portfolio.
4. **Donation reform:** faster disclosure (reversing the 2025 extension), a much smaller anonymous allowance, and a cap on donation size. Lobbying rules only cover the meeting; donations cover the relationship behind it.

**The politics:** in a September 2026 survey of parties, the Greens and TOP support a register and stand-down periods; Labour, NZ First and Te Pāti Māori gave general or partial support. National and ACT didn't respond. The Ministry of Justice's reform work was halted under this government. If the election produces a government that needs the Greens, TOP or NZ First, lobbying rules could plausibly come up in coalition negotiations.

*Your own view: whether a register is worth the compliance cost, or whether better diaries are enough.*

## Back-up questions

### How will the media landscape evolve, and what will it mean for political engagement?

**Fewer outlets, more fragmented audiences, and more of the political conversation run by people with a stake in it.**

- Newshub closed in 2024; RNZ's funding was cut by $18 million in Budget 2025, and $6.4 million over four years went to local, council and court reporting. Social media is now the main news source for 19% of people.
- **Polling is the clearest example.** Nearly three times as many published polls this term as in 2017–20, but media outlets commission only about a quarter of them. The rest come from Roy Morgan, the Taxpayers' Union and polls commissioned by corporate or party clients and released when it suits them. Voters see more data and less independent journalism about it.
- Big parties are losing share as the audience fragments. Labour and National together are polling about 56%, down from the mid-80s in 2008 and 2018. A fragmented media makes it easier for smaller parties to reach voters directly, and harder for anyone to build a shared picture of the country.

*Your own view: whether the RNZ–TVNZ mix is sustainable.*

### How can governments provide certainty for business while staying responsive?

**Certainty comes from cross-party agreement, not from any one government.** Under MMP, governments change and coalitions shift. Our forecasting work found polling behaves like a random walk, so no business can know three years out who will govern. Labour and National are currently tied. Policy that only one bloc supports is at risk of being reversed in the next term. That has happened to a lot of policy in recent years, in both directions: oil and gas exploration, Three Waters, fair pay agreements, the clean car discount.

What gives certainty:

- **Independent institutions with cross-party mandates:** the Reserve Bank, the Infrastructure Commission, the Climate Change Commission.
- **Rules about how policy changes,** not promises that it won't: consultation periods, transition timelines, grandparenting existing investments.
- **Building the other side into the design** of policy meant to last 20 years, such as infrastructure pipelines and emissions settings.

Responsiveness and certainty conflict less than people think. Firms can live with policy that changes predictably. What hurts them is reversal every three years.

### Why do business confidence surveys attract so much attention?

**They come out early, they're simple, and both sides of politics can use them. They're also less informative than the attention suggests.**

- They're monthly and quick. The ANZ Business Outlook comes out within days of the month ending, while GDP takes about three months. In a news cycle, the first number available gets the coverage.
- They give business a voice in a form that looks objective, and opposition parties quote them whenever they're bad.

What the data says (OECD composite indices, 1991–2026, excluding the Covid years):

- **Business confidence predicts next quarter's GDP growth no better than consumer confidence does** (r=0.26 for both).
- **Business confidence doesn't move votes.** Quarterly changes in business confidence don't predict changes in government support (r=−0.04; Granger test not significant). Consumer confidence does (Granger p=0.04).
- **It's partly partisan.** It averages about 2 points higher under National than Labour, and it fell after Labour took office in both 1999 and 2017.
- **Right now** the OECD business confidence index is above its long-run average (102.9 in September 2026; ANZ's headline confidence measure is +52). The government is polling near its lows anyway. Business mood and voter mood aren't the same thing.

## If challenged

- **Poll commissioners:** counts are polls listed on Wikipedia's opinion-polling pages. The 2017–20 list is shorter (46 polls), so compare shares rather than counts. "Media outlet" covers 1News, Newshub, RNZ, Stuff and The Post. Talbot Mills' corporate poll is counted as party or corporate.
- **Taxpayers' Union–Curia house effect:** each poll is compared with other pollsters' polls within 30 days, November 2023–October 2026. Right bloc +0.7, National +0.5, Green −1.4. Roy Morgan has Labour −4.0 and Green +2.6.
- **Business confidence:** OECD composite business confidence index (amplitude-adjusted, 100 = long-run average, standard deviation about 1.9). It's not the ANZ headline number. The changes around changes of government rest on four events.
- **Trust in news:** the JMAD figures are from a single online panel survey of 1,040 people. A 5-point rise is just outside the margin of error, and the Acumen survey supports it. The partisan breakdown is by recalled 2023 party vote.
- **"But we're one of the least corrupt countries in the world":** the Corruption Perceptions Index draws on expert and business surveys of perceived bribery and abuse of public office. A country with permissive donation and lobbying laws can score well because it has defined fewer practices as corrupt. Donation figures are the Electoral Commission's 2026 limits.
- **"Concentration is actually falling":** true, slightly, and the Commerce Commission says so. Its reading is that market share has moved from the very largest firms to other established firms, while new entrants find it harder to get in. The NZ Initiative criticised the report's industry rankings as too broad. The entry and exit trend is the robust part.
- **Inflation figures:** Stats NZ CPI, annual change by quarter. 7.3% in June 2022 was the highest since 7.6% in June 1990. The doubling times use the rule of 70.
- **Ministerial diaries:** these record disclosed entries, not meetings. Absence from a diary doesn't mean no contact, and diary practice varies by minister and period.
