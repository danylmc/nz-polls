# Traditional governing pairs at elections

**Compiled 6 October 2026.** The combined vote for each country's two selected, long-established governing parties is compared at the election nearest 2005, the election nearest 2017 (earlier election used for a tie), and the latest completed national lower-house election. Figures are rounded to one decimal; changes are percentage points. Australia and Ireland use first preferences; Germany uses second votes. These are election results, not polling estimates.

**Answer:** NZ's 16.3-point fall from 2017 is substantial but not unique. The UK fell farther from its 2017 peak. Denmark and Portugal also show large declines from nearby elections; Australia, Austria and Norway declined more moderately. Germany, Ireland, Sweden and Greece had already lost much of their traditional-pair vote by that point. Relative to the mid-2000s, Germany, Austria, Denmark, Ireland, Portugal and Greece show larger losses than NZ. Greece and Spain recovered after earlier lows, while Canada moved decisively toward its traditional pair.

The UK’s 2017 result was an unusually high two-party election; comparing only that year makes its subsequent fall look particularly large. Ireland's two parties are historical rivals with broadly similar centre-right positions, so its row tracks the old governing duopoly rather than a left/right split. Canada's Liberals are a centrist party, not a labour party. The rows measure *named parties*, not whole ideological blocs or governing-coalition votes.

Denmark's Venstre is a centre-right liberal party despite its name, which means “left.” The Denmark figures are calculated from official party vote counts over all valid votes, then rounded; changes use the unrounded ratios. ### Other cases worth adding

Finland is informative, but its [Centre Party has long been a major governing party](https://finland.fi/life-society/parliamentarism-in-finland/), so SDP + National Coalition alone understates the traditional governing core. [Italy](https://elezionistorico.interno.gov.it/) and [Belgium](https://elections.fgov.be/) need party-family or alliance definitions across party changes and, in Belgium, separate language communities. Japan is a potential comparison, but the opposition parties have repeatedly reorganised; a fixed LDP-plus-opposition-party series would be misleading.

### Netherlands: an exploratory comparison

| Election | PvdA + VVD (2025: joint GroenLinks–PvdA + VVD) | Including CDA |
|---|---:|---:|
| 2006 | 35.9% | 62.4% |
| 2017 | 27.0% | 39.4% |
| 2025 | 27.0% | 38.8% |

The Dutch two-party pair fell 8.9 points between 2006 and 2017. The apparently flat 2017–2025 comparison is **not like for like**: PvdA and GroenLinks ran a joint list in 2025, so its 12.79% includes voters who would previously have chosen GroenLinks. Nor does PvdA + VVD capture the full old Dutch governing core: CDA was historically another major party. Counting all three shows a much larger earlier decline, but the 2025 joint-list caveat still applies. Sources: [2006 Groningen University, using Kiesraad results](https://www.rug.nl/research/dnpp/verkiezingen/tweede-kamer/uitslagen-tweede-kamerverkiezingen-1986-2021), [2017 parliamentary election results](https://www.parlement.com/tweede-kamerverkiezingen-2017), [2025 parliamentary election results](https://www.parlement.com/tweede-kamerverkiezingen-2025), and the [2025 Kiesraad certification](https://www.kiesraad.nl/actueel/nieuws/2025/11/7/kiesraad-uitslag-tweede-kamerverkiezing-betrouwbaar). The underlying values are in [`../data/purple_vote_netherlands_context.csv`](../data/purple_vote_netherlands_context.csv).

[France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opinion_polling_for_the_2027_French_presidential_election) is excluded because party labels, legislative alliances, and presidential candidate votes cannot be made into one consistent lower-house series for the old Socialist/Republicans pair.
