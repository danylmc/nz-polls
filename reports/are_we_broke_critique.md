# Critique: "Are We Really Broke?" — A Review of the Presentation's Arguments

## Summary

The presentation makes a timely and largely defensible empirical case against the National-led government's "fiscal emergency" framing. Its core claims — that NZ government debt is low by OECD standards, that private debt is the real structural risk, and that the 30% net debt/GDP ceiling is a political choice rather than an economic necessity — are well-supported by the data presented. However, the deck argues too hard for comfort. It conflates government solvency with public service adequacy, undersells a genuine external vulnerability, and dismisses legitimate rationales for fiscal buffers without engaging them. The result is effective advocacy that would be more persuasive as honest analysis.

---

## What the Presentation Gets Right

**Government debt is genuinely low.** NZ's net public debt sits near the low end of the OECD distribution, interest costs as a share of government income are under 2%, and the government's financial balance sheet shows positive net worth. These are real facts, not spin.

**The 30% target is a political choice.** No economic theorem specifies 30% net debt/GDP as the correct ceiling for a country in NZ's position. The current government chose this number as a fiscal anchor; it can be questioned.

**Private debt is the structural risk.** The chart showing public debt falling from 1992 while private debt ballooned to 150%+ of GDP is the presentation's strongest material. The debt problem in NZ is predominantly a private-sector mortgage phenomenon, not a government profligacy story.

**Household wealth is concentrated, not diffuse.** Acknowledging the quintile distribution — bottom quintile at -$4M median net worth, top quintile at $1.37M — is to the presentation's credit, even if it then fails to draw the right conclusions from it.

---

## Where the Arguments Break Down

### 1. Stock vs. Flow: A Solvent Government Can Still Face Real Constraints

The opening slide compares government debt liabilities against equity and investment share assets to argue the Crown is "net rich." But the assets in question — SOEs like Meridian Energy and Genesis, the NZ Super Fund, ACC's investment portfolio — are not operationally fungible with government expenditure. You cannot sell a power company to fund a pay round for nurses. A government can simultaneously have a strong balance sheet and a structural operating deficit that requires real spending choices. Solvency and fiscal adequacy are different questions. The presentation conflates them throughout.

### 2. Household Wealth Is High Because House Prices Are High

The claim that NZ households have the second-highest net financial worth in the OECD (behind only the United States) is empirically correct. But the presentation's own evidence shows why: the annual change in household assets chart demonstrates that the surge in household wealth from 2019 to 2022 was driven overwhelmingly by non-financial asset appreciation — that is, residential land values. This wealth is:

- **Illiquid.** A household cannot spend equity in its home on groceries.
- **Concentrated.** As the quintile chart shows, the wealth is held almost entirely by the top 20% of households.
- **Circular as an argument.** Pointing to high household wealth as evidence that people are not really under financial stress ignores that housing wealth is expensive to access — especially for the renters and mortgage-holders whose cost-of-living stress is real regardless of aggregate balance sheet statistics.

High aggregate household net worth and genuine household financial stress are not mutually exclusive. The cost-of-living crisis that drove polling shifts from 2021 to 2023 was experienced by people at the bottom of the wealth distribution, whose situation is not improved by the fact that wealthy homeowners have large paper gains on their properties.

### 3. The "Landlords Are Indebted" Framing Overstates Its Case

The slide identifying NZ's non-financial corporations as the most indebted in the OECD, and labelling them "landlord firms," is doing considerable analytical work on limited evidence. The non-financial corporate sector in NZ includes:

- **Dairy farming.** NZ's dairy sector borrowed heavily against inflated land values during the commodity boom of the 2000s. Much of the corporate debt in this sector reflects farm purchases from retiring farmers who then appear as wealthy households — the same wealth transfer the presentation celebrates.
- **Infrastructure and utilities.** Capital-intensive businesses with long asset lives and stable cash flows carry structural debt that looks bad on a net financial worth chart but is serviceably financed by operating income.
- **Residential property trusts and LTCs.** Many NZ property investors do operate through corporate structures, but many others operate through trusts or personal names and appear in the household sector, not the corporate sector.

Labelling the entire non-financial corporate sector's negative net financial worth as a "landlord" phenomenon is a narrative convenience that the data do not cleanly support.

### 4. The Net International Investment Position Is Being Undersold

The slide on NZ's Net International Investment Position (NIIP) frames the current position of approximately -45% of GDP as good news relative to the -75% trough in 2009. This framing is misleading by selective benchmarking.

A NIIP of -45% of GDP means NZ owes the rest of the world the equivalent of nearly half a year's economic output in net terms — a structural current account deficit financed by persistent inflows of foreign capital. For a small, open economy with a commodity-dependent export base and a freely floating currency, this represents genuine external vulnerability. It means:

- NZ's financing costs are sensitive to global credit conditions regardless of how clean the domestic government balance sheet looks.
- A deterioration in global risk appetite or a commodity shock can cause rapid capital outflows and currency depreciation, raising the real cost of external debt.
- The external position does not appear on the government balance sheet but constrains the government's macro-stabilisation options.

The improvement from the 2009 trough is real but the baseline was exceptional. Being less exposed than your worst moment is not the same as being in a structurally comfortable position.

### 5. "No Good Reason" Is a Rhetorical Move, Not an Argument

The slide dismissing the 30% net debt/GDP target as having "no good reason" is the presentation's weakest moment. The actual case for debt ceilings in NZ's context includes:

- **Shock absorption capacity.** NZ is exposed to idiosyncratic large shocks — the Canterbury earthquakes, COVID-19, Cyclone Gabrielle — that require rapid, substantial fiscal expansion. The 2020 response was possible precisely because NZ entered the pandemic from a low-debt position. Whether the buffer has been rebuilt adequately is a legitimate question.
- **Small open economy constraints.** Large economies (the US, Japan) can sustain higher debt-to-GDP ratios partly because their currencies are reserve assets and they face virtually no external financing constraint. NZ faces both.
- **Credibility and credit ratings.** NZ's AAA credit rating keeps borrowing costs low and is explicitly tied to fiscal settings. The rating agencies do look at debt trajectories, not just current levels.

None of this means 30% is the correct number, or that the current pace of fiscal consolidation is optimal. Those are defensible positions. But dismissing the rationale for any debt ceiling with "no good reason" does not engage the argument — it avoids it.

### 6. The Distributional Insight Is Acknowledged and Then Abandoned

One of the more honest moments in the presentation is the household net worth quintile chart, which shows extreme wealth concentration: the bottom quintile has negative net worth, the second quintile barely positive, and the vast majority of aggregate household wealth sits with the top 20%. The slide headline reads "Some households are really, really rich. Some are poor."

But this finding does no work in the presentation's conclusions. The argument that NZ is not "broke" because household net financial worth is high as a share of GDP only holds for households that are actually wealthy. For the bottom 40% of households, the aggregate statistic is irrelevant to their financial position. A complete treatment of this material would distinguish between the fiscal position of the government (genuinely sound), the financial position of wealthy households (strong), and the financial position of lower-income households and renters (under genuine stress). The presentation conflates all three under the heading "we're rich."

### 7. No Engagement with Future Fiscal Pressures

The presentation is entirely backward-looking: historical debt levels, current balance sheets, comparison to the 1980s peak. It does not address:

- **Demographic change.** NZ Superannuation costs will increase significantly as the population ages. The current fiscal position looks better than it will in 15 years.
- **Deferred infrastructure investment.** Years of under-investment in water infrastructure, transport, housing-enabling infrastructure, and climate adaptation represent real future spending obligations not captured in current balance sheet statistics.
- **Climate adaptation.** The fiscal cost of managed retreat, flood defence, and infrastructure resilience in a country with NZ's geographic exposure is material and not priced into current debt projections.

A government can have low current debt and face a genuinely difficult fiscal trajectory if its structural expenditure pressures exceed its revenue base. The presentation's argument that "we're not broke" speaks only to the present moment.

---

## Overall Assessment

"Are We Really Broke?" is a well-executed response to a government framing that probably does deserve to be challenged. The core empirical case — NZ government debt is low, private debt is the real structural story, household wealth is high — is sound, and the data presentation is of high quality.

But the presentation is advocacy dressed as analysis. Its most significant failure is treating solvency as sufficient for adequacy: a government can simultaneously have a clean balance sheet and be under-spending on health, housing, and public services in ways that cause real harm to real people. The distributional analysis is the most interesting part of the deck and the least developed. A presentation willing to engage seriously with the bottom-quintile net worth data, the external vulnerability, and the demographic fiscal trajectory would be considerably more persuasive — because it would be harder to dismiss.

The "we're not broke, we're rich" framing is politically useful but analytically incomplete. NZ's fiscal challenges are real; they are just not primarily located where the current government claims they are.
