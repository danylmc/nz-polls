# Election panel: answers from the NZ polling data

*As of 14 October 2026*

Draft answers for the panel, each about 60–90 seconds spoken. Questions 1, 2 and 5 rest on the project's data: 1,020 published polls (1993–2026), 34 waves of the Ipsos Issues Monitor (2018–2026), NZ Election Study surveys and economic series. Questions 3, 4 and 6 are mostly policy and judgement; for those the answer gives the angle the data supports, and *italic notes* mark where you need to add your own view.

## 1. Which issue has the emotional power to decide votes?

**Cost of living, and it isn't close.** It has topped the Ipsos Issues Monitor every wave since 2022 and is now at 63%, its highest reading. It also actually moves votes. Across eight years of Ipsos data, cost-of-living concern alone explains about two-thirds of the variation in how people rate the government, and that rating is the best single predictor of the incumbent's vote we've found.

The others behave differently:

- **Fuel prices** are cost of living at its most visible. Petrol has a stronger short-term effect on incumbent support than mortgage rates, and it shows up the same month. People see the price every time they fill up.
- **Health** jumped from 38% to 44% in the latest wave. But once you control for era, health concern doesn't reliably move votes to any party.
- **Housing** concern has fallen from around 50% in 2018–21 to 19%, the lowest in the series. House prices show no net effect on incumbent support: homeowners feeling richer cancels out renters feeling locked out.
- **Unemployment** is the one to watch. It was 3% in 2022 and is 21% now. When concern about jobs or the economy rises from one wave to the next, votes tend to move to Labour.

On "recovering but still feels difficult": how people feel beats the official statistics. GDP, unemployment and inflation figures barely predict polling, while consumer confidence and government approval predict it strongly. The recovery has to be felt before it counts.

## 2. Are voters moving to minor parties because they're attractive, or because the majors aren't giving them a reason to come home?

**The data points mostly to the second.** Normally New Zealand politics is a seesaw: when the government drops, the main opposition picks up almost all of it. That has broken down this year. Since mid-year National has slipped about 2 points and Labour about 5, at the same time.

| Period | Labour + National combined |
|---|---:|
| 2008 | 84% |
| 2018 | 87% |
| 2024–25 | 64–65% |
| Polls since September 2026 | 56% |

Ipsos asks which party is best to handle each issue. Labour leads on all of the top issues, yet polls 28%. On cost of living, only about half the public names either major party. A competence lead that doesn't turn into votes suggests voters aren't hearing a reason to come back, rather than being pulled away.

To be fair to the minor parties, the Greens are at record highs (around 15%) and TOP is at about 6%. Something is attracting those voters, and the data can't separate attraction from rejection at the individual level.

**What it says about New Zealand:** we're converging on the international norm, not becoming an outlier. Even at 56%, our two big parties hold more of the vote than their counterparts in the UK (46%), Australia (49%) or Germany (33%). And don't assume minor parties get squeezed before election day: tested across every election since 1996, minor-party support doesn't fade in the final weeks under MMP.

## 3. Which policy difference would you model at the board table?

*The data can't pick a policy; add the specific policy differences you'd name.*

**Model the coalition, not the manifesto.** Labour and National are tied. Our forecasting work found polling behaves like a random walk: nothing beats "assume today's numbers hold", and that still misses by about 2.6 points per party on average. That's well within the gap between possible governments.

So the government will be decided by the Greens (15%), NZ First (10%), ACT (10%) and possibly TOP (6%). The policies worth modelling are the ones a minor party is likely to make a bottom line in coalition talks. A policy the kingmaker insists on is more likely to be implemented than one from a major party's own list.

## 4. The supermarket debate: what's the bigger signal for business?

*The data doesn't cover the supermarket debate itself, but it explains why the parties have converged.*

**It's the cost-of-living effect at work.** With 63% of people naming cost of living as a top issue, and that concern driving government approval more than anything else we've measured, every party has a strong incentive to be seen acting on it. Groceries, like petrol, are a cost people see every week, and the most visible costs have the fastest political effects.

The lesson for business is that ideology isn't the main protection. Any sector that becomes the public face of cost of living becomes politically exposed, whoever is in government. The risk follows how visible your prices are, not which party is in power.

## 5. What is the media giving too much or too little oxygen to?

Comparing Streem's media top three (cost of living & housing, infrastructure, foreign affairs) with what the public tells Ipsos:

- **Too little: health.** It's the second-biggest public concern (44%), up 6 points in the latest wave, and isn't in the media top three.
- **Too little: jobs.** Unemployment concern has risen about sevenfold since 2022, and it's the issue that moves votes to Labour from wave to wave.
- **Probably too much: housing**, at least as a stand-alone story. Public concern about housing is at a series low.
- **Infrastructure and foreign affairs** don't appear among the issues the public ranks highest in the Ipsos data we hold. They may matter for business, but they aren't moving voters.
- **Too much: single polls.** About two-thirds of extreme polls revert toward the average in the next reading, and big poll-to-poll jumps tend to partly reverse. "Party X surges" is usually noise until several polls confirm it.

## 6. When does a CEO intervention land rather than read as lobbying?

*This is mostly your judgement; the data adds one point.*

**Voters read everything through a partisan filter.** In the NZ Election Study, government and opposition voters looking at the same economy rated it differently, by about two-thirds of a point on the survey scale, in every year. Values and ideology predict vote choice more than judgements of competence do.

A CEO intervention that sounds like one side's argument will be sorted into that side and discounted by the other half of the audience. Interventions that hold up tend to be specific, cost the company something, and concern its own operating reality rather than which party should govern.

## If challenged

- The minor-party comparison uses unweighted averages of published polls, not a modelled average. The project's weighted average gives a similar figure (56.8% Labour + National on 6 October).
- The cross-country figures are descriptive: electoral systems, pollsters and timing differ.
- Left out deliberately: the "myopic voting" result (election-year growth matters most). It's only suggestive (p≈0.10, nine elections).
- Household disposable income hasn't been tested directly. The closest evidence, from the Election Study, is that voters' views of the national economy predict their vote better than their views of their own finances.
