## Central Thesis: Three Tensions

NZ as a case study where three dynamics coexist:

1. **Scarcity politics** — NZ's political volatility is downstream of supply-side failure. Distributional coalitions block abundance; voters punish incumbents for scarcity they can't fix.
2. **Democratic shallowness** — Retrospective pocketbook voting, partisan perceptual screens, and myopic retrospection show voters reacting, not deliberating. Democracy is functional but shallow.
3. **Institutional containment** — Despite (1) and (2), NZ's institutions absorb shocks. MMP channels discontent into coalition bargaining. NZ sits in the "narrow corridor" and stays there.

The document holds all three in tension — not resolving them into a single narrative, but making each available as a lens for writing.

## Theoretical Frameworks

### Hannah Arendt
- **The Human Condition (1958):** Distinction between labour (biological necessity), work (fabrication), and action (political speech/deed in public). Modern society reduces politics to administration of necessity — "the social" replaces "the political."
- **Relevance:** NZ voters as labourers, not actors. Pocketbook voting (mortgage rates, petrol) is politics-as-necessity-management. The causal chain (costs → salience → approval → vote) is the mechanism by which "the social" colonises "the political." Voters respond to material conditions, not to deliberation about the common good.
- **Key tension:** Arendt would see retrospective voting (mean r=0.376) and the perceptual screen (+0.68 gap) as evidence that political action has been replaced by consumer reaction. But MMP's split-ticket voting (~30%) could be read as residual political judgement.

### Ezra Klein & Derek Thompson (Abundance Agenda)
- **Core idea:** Progressive goals (housing, clean energy, infrastructure) fail not from lack of demand but from supply-side blockages — permitting, NIMBYism, regulatory capture, vetocracy. The solution is building more, not redistributing existing scarcity.
- **Relevance:** NZ's cost-of-living salience (explains 74% of approval variance) is a direct consequence of failure to build. Housing unaffordability, infrastructure deficits, and low productivity are the material base of political discontent. The pocketbook findings are symptoms; the abundance failure is the disease.
- **Key data needed:** NZ building consents (supply response), OECD price-to-income ratios (comparative affordability), NZ productivity (output per hour vs OECD).

### Mancur Olson
- **The Logic of Collective Action (1965):** Small, concentrated interest groups organise effectively; large, diffuse groups (consumers, renters, the public) don't. Over time, democratic societies accumulate distributional coalitions that slow growth.
- **The Rise and Decline of Nations (1982):** Institutional sclerosis — stable democracies accumulate rent-seeking coalitions. Long-stable democracies (like NZ, uninterrupted since 1856) should show maximum sclerosis.
- **Relevance:** NZ's housing crisis is a textbook Olson problem. Homeowners (concentrated, organised, vote at higher rates) block intensification. Renters and future buyers (diffuse, disorganised) bear the cost. The regime-dependence finding (same indicator, opposite meaning under different governments) suggests both major parties are captured by different coalitions rather than solving supply.
- **Key evidence:** House prices politically neutral (wealth cancels affordability, r=-0.069) — this IS the Olson equilibrium. Both sides of the distributional coalition are politically represented, producing stasis.

### Francis Fukuyama
- **Political Order and Political Decay (2014):** Three pillars — state capacity, rule of law, democratic accountability. Decay occurs when one pillar weakens or when "repatrimonialisation" (capture by interest groups) corrodes state capacity.
- **Identity (2018):** Modern politics increasingly organised around identity (dignity, recognition) rather than economic interest. Left-right economic cleavage gives way to identity cleavage.
- **Relevance:** NZ's dealignment findings map directly. Class-based voting weakening (partial dealignment), Maori identity strengthening as dominant predictor (OR=0.14-0.39), age cleavage reversing — this is Fukuyama's identity thesis in data. State capacity question: NZ scores high on governance indicators but struggles to deliver housing and infrastructure (Fukuyama's "getting to Denmark" vs staying there).
- **Key evidence:** L-R positional voting strengthening (r=0.533→0.760) without polarisation suggests ideological sorting, not identity-driven polarisation. NZ may be a counter-case to Fukuyama's pessimism — identity (Maori) is entering politics through institutional channels (Te Pati Maori, co-governance) rather than through populist disruption.

### Acemoglu, Robinson et al.
- **Why Nations Fail (2012):** Inclusive vs extractive institutions. Inclusive institutions (broad participation, pluralism, property rights, creative destruction) drive prosperity. Extractive institutions concentrate power and wealth.
- **The Narrow Corridor (2019):** Liberty requires a balance between state and society. Too much state → despotism. Too much society → failed state. The "corridor" is narrow and requires constant contestation to stay in.
- **Relevance:** NZ as a narrow-corridor success story. MMP is an inclusive institution par excellence — it channels minor party support into coalition bargaining rather than exclusion. No third-party squeeze (p=0.682). Split-ticket voting as institutional design working as intended. Poll accuracy (winner correct 9/10) as evidence of a functioning information system. But: housing sclerosis and low productivity suggest extractive elements (homeowner wealth protection) within an otherwise inclusive framework.

### 2. The Scarcity Trap (~800 words)
*Primary: Olson, Klein/Thompson. Secondary: Fukuyama (state capacity)*

**Argument:** NZ's political volatility is a rational response to material scarcity caused by institutional sclerosis. The causal chain (costs → salience → approval → vote) is what happens when a democracy fails to build.

**Evidence from existing analyses:**
- Causal chain: mortgage 68% mediated, petrol 49% mediated through approval
- Cost-of-living salience → 74% of approval variance
- Mortgage rates Granger-causal (F=13.8), petrol Granger-causal (F=12.0)
- House prices null (r=-0.069) — the Olson equilibrium in data
- Regime dependence: rate rises hurt Labour, rate falls hurt National — neither party solves supply
- Issue ownership asymmetry: cost-of-living activates National, but National in power faces same cost pressures

**New comparative data (to download and cite):**
- NZ price-to-income ratio vs OECD average (how extreme is NZ's affordability problem?)
- NZ building consents trend (is supply responding to demand?)
- NZ labour productivity vs OECD (is the economy generating enough output per hour?)
- NZ housing supply elasticity (Caldera Sanchez & Johansson estimate — cite from PDF)

**Theoretical framing:**
- Olson: NZ is a 170-year-old stable democracy — maximum distributional coalition accumulation. Homeowners are the archetypal Olson group: concentrated interest, high political participation, captured both parties.
- Klein/Thompson: The abundance diagnosis — NZ doesn't lack resources or technology, it lacks permission to build. RMA (Resource Management Act) as vetocracy.
- Fukuyama: State capacity paradox — NZ ranks top-5 globally on governance indicators but cannot deliver housing or infrastructure. High-quality institutions that have been captured.

### 3. The Accountability Paradox (~800 words)
*Primary: Arendt, Fiorina. Secondary: Achen & Bartels*

**Argument:** NZ voters hold governments accountable — but for the wrong things, in the wrong way, on the wrong timescale. This is democracy functioning as consumer feedback, not as political action.

**Evidence from existing analyses:**
- Retrospective voting in all 10 elections (mean r=0.376) — voters punish past, don't invest in future
- Prospective loses 4/5 head-to-head tests — forward-looking evaluation is weaker
- Partisan perceptual screen (+0.68 gap) — voters see economy through partisan lenses
- Myopic retrospection: election-year GDP (r=+0.588) vs early-term (r=-0.114) — only recent performance matters
- Random walk: no indicator predicts vote changes — current polls already incorporate everything
- Sociotropic > egotropic — voters respond to national narrative, not personal experience
- Government approval as master variable (R²=0.675) — the signal voters use is itself a perception

**New comparative data:**
- OECD trust in government (NZ 46% vs OECD 39%) — NZ trusts more than average but approval is at record low. Trust ≠ approval.

**Theoretical framing:**
- Arendt: The causal chain is "the social" displacing "the political." Voters don't deliberate about the common good — they react to material conditions (mortgage rates, petrol prices) through an affect channel (approval). This is labour, not action. The public sphere has been reduced to a feedback loop.
- But: split-ticket voting (~30%) and the strengthening of L-R positional voting (r=0.533→0.760) suggest some voters ARE making ideological judgements, not just consumer reactions. Arendt's framework may overstate the passivity.
- Achen & Bartels (2016): Myopic retrospection evidence (suggestive, N=9) is consistent with their claim that voters are "blind retrospectors" — they don't even evaluate the full term, just the recent period. If confirmed, this means accountability is even weaker than retrospective voting alone suggests.
- The perceptual screen is the deepest challenge: if partisans see different economies, accountability is partly circular (vote → perception → vote).

### 4. Identity, Realignment, and the New Cleavage (~600 words)
*Primary: Fukuyama (Identity). Secondary: Ford & Jennings*

**Argument:** NZ is experiencing realignment, not dealignment. The old class cleavage is weakening; the new cleavage is ethnic identity. This is consistent with Fukuyama's prediction but NZ's institutional response differs from the US/European pattern.

**Evidence from existing analyses:**
- Dealignment NOT confirmed (pseudo-R² stable/increasing 0.032→0.044)
- Maori identity: dominant predictor (OR=0.14-0.39 for National vote), strengthening
- Age reversal: young→National (1990s) to old→National (2005+), now +20pp gap
- Gender and education effects weakening toward zero
- Class (income) dealignment partial — income-vote relationship weakening
- L-R positional voting strengthening without distribution polarising — sorting, not polarisation

**Theoretical framing:**
- Fukuyama (Identity): Maori identity entering politics through institutional channels (Te Pati Maori, Maori seats, co-governance debates) rather than through populist disruption. NZ's institutional design accommodates identity politics better than US/European systems.
- Contrast with Fukuyama's pessimism: NZ shows that identity can be channelled through inclusive institutions. MMP's proportional representation gives ethnic parties parliamentary representation without requiring ethnic majority.
- Ford & Jennings (2020): NZ confirms their "realignment" thesis — old cleavages (class, gender) weakening, new cleavage (ethnicity) strengthening, while overall demographic predictive power remains stable.

### 5. The Narrow Corridor: Why NZ Doesn't Break (~600 words)
*Primary: Acemoglu & Robinson. Secondary: Fukuyama (Political Order)*

**Argument:** NZ sits firmly in the "narrow corridor" between state capacity and societal mobilisation. Its institutions channel discontent into orderly alternation rather than populist breakdown. The evidence for this is scattered across all 30 analyses.

**Evidence from existing analyses:**
- MMP mechanics: no third-party squeeze (p=0.682), split-ticket voting (~30%), minor party viability (18.6% combined)
- Poll accuracy: winner correct 9/10, no shy Tory effect, polls converge near election day
- Orderly alternation: universal incumbent decay (-0.114 pp/month) produces regular turnover
- Mean reversion (67% of extreme polls revert) — the system self-corrects
- Zero-sum bloc dynamics: discontent transfers from incumbent major party to opposition major party, not to anti-system parties
- NZ First as safety valve: episodic populist energy channelled through coalition participation, support collapses in government
- Crime salience tracks actual crime (r=0.569) — the information system works for some issues

**New comparative data:**
- World Governance Indicators: NZ vs OECD peers across 6 dimensions (1996-2023)
- Freedom House scores: NZ trajectory (should be flat at top — confirming corridor stability)

**Theoretical framing:**
- Acemoglu & Robinson: NZ's corridor is wide because of dual mechanisms — state capacity is high (WGI scores) AND societal mobilisation is channelled through institutions (MMP, Maori seats, coalition bargaining). Neither side dominates.
- The NZ First pattern is key: populist energy enters parliament, gets absorbed into coalition government, and dissipates. This is the corridor working — society mobilises, the state accommodates, extremes are moderated.
- But: the scarcity trap (Section 2) shows the corridor has limits. Inclusive institutions don't guarantee inclusive outcomes. Housing sclerosis within the corridor = extractive dynamics inside an inclusive shell.

### 6. Distributional Coalitions and the Abundance Failure (~600 words)
*Primary: Olson. Secondary: Klein/Thompson, Acemoglu*

**Argument:** NZ's housing and infrastructure failures are a specific case of Olson's institutional sclerosis. The political system accurately reflects voter preferences (Section 5) but those preferences are shaped by distributional coalitions that block abundance (Section 2). This is the mechanism by which inclusive institutions produce exclusionary outcomes.

**Evidence from existing analyses:**
- House prices politically neutral (r=-0.069) — homeowners and renters cancel each other out
- Rental inflation associational only (r=-0.731 but fails Granger) — renters don't have political voice proportional to their suffering
- Mortgage rates causal but regime-dependent — neither party has incentive to fix supply
- Minor parties insulated from pocketbook cycle — Green and ACT voters don't swing on costs, reducing pressure for supply-side reform
- Issue ownership: cost-of-living helps National into power, but National in power faces same constraints

**New comparative data:**
- NZ building consents over time (has supply responded to demand signals?)
- NZ productivity vs OECD (is low productivity the deeper sclerosis?)
- OECD price-to-income ratio (how does NZ compare?)

**Theoretical framing:**
- Olson: The null house price finding IS the theory. Both sides of the distributional coalition are politically represented. Homeowners benefit from scarcity (wealth effect). Renters suffer but are less organised, younger, more mobile, lower turnout. The system equilibrates politically while failing economically.
- Klein/Thompson: NZ's RMA and planning system as vetocracy. Each consent holder has Olsonian concentrated interest in blocking competition. The abundance agenda requires overcoming these veto points.
- Acemoglu: Creative destruction is the engine of inclusive institutions. NZ's housing market lacks creative destruction — incumbents (homeowners) are protected at the expense of entrants (renters, young families). This is an extractive pocket within the inclusive corridor.
