1. Thermostatic Policy Responsiveness (Wlezien 1995; Soroka & Wlezien 2010)
You've already got a thermostatic correction in polling levels, but the original theory is about policy: when government policy moves left, public preferences shift right (and vice versa). You could test this by coding each government's policy direction (using manifesto data from the Comparative Manifesto Project, which covers NZ) and checking whether the public's left-right self-placement in the NZES moves in the opposite direction during that government's tenure. Your ideology.md data showing stable mean L-R placement is suggestive but doesn't directly test the policy-response mechanism.
2. Issue Ownership Theory (Petrocik 1996; Egan 2013)
The idea that parties "own" certain issues and benefit when those issues are salient. You've already found this for NZ First and immigration (Rule 16). You could extend it systematically: does National benefit when crime or economic management is salient? Does Labour benefit when health or education is prominent? The NZES includes "most important issue" questions in most waves. Cross-referencing which issues respondents name with their vote choice and switching behaviour would let you test whether issue salience predicts vote flows in the direction the theory expects, and whether "owned" issues drive cross-bloc switching more than within-bloc movement.
3. Economic Retrospection vs Prospection (Fiorina 1981; MacKuen, Erikson & Stimson 1992)
Your perception data captures retrospective economic assessments, but the NZES also asks about expected future economic conditions in several waves. The "banker's" model (prospective voting) argues voters are forward-looking, while the "peasant" model (retrospective) says they reward or punish based on past performance. You could directly test which matters more for NZ vote choice — and whether the answer differs by education level or political sophistication, as the literature predicts.
4. Myopic vs Cumulative Retrospection (Healy & Lenz 2014; Achen & Bartels 2016)
Related but distinct: do voters evaluate the entire term or just the most recent period? Achen and Bartels argue voters are "myopic," overweighting the election-year economy. Your quarterly economic data matched to polls lets you test this directly — does the economic indicator from Q1 of a government's term predict incumbent support at election time, or only the final year's economy? Your distributed lag model is partway there, but you could structure it explicitly around this question by comparing early-term vs late-term economic conditions as predictors of the election result.
5. Valence Politics (Stokes 1963; Clarke et al. 2004, 2009)
The theory that elections are less about ideological positioning and more about competence — voters choose the party they perceive as most capable of managing agreed-upon goals (economic management, public services). Your NZES data likely includes leader evaluations and competence ratings. You could test whether perceived competence explains more variance in vote choice than left-right distance, and whether competence evaluations mediate the economic voting effects you've already found. The "valence" interpretation would predict that economic conditions affect votes through competence perceptions rather than directly.
6. The Cost of Ruling (Paldam 1991; Nannestad & Paldam 2002)
Your incumbent fatigue finding (Rule 1, -1.56 pts/year) is essentially this. But Paldam's comparative work identifies specific moderators: the cost of ruling is steeper for coalition governments than single-party governments, steeper during economic downturns, and steeper in proportional systems. You could test all three: is fatigue faster under coalition governments (1996-98, 1999-2008, 2017-2020) than under majority/near-majority ones (2008-2017, 2020-2023)? Does the fatigue rate interact with your economic indicators?
7. Dealignment and Realignment (Dalton 2000, 2008; Ford & Jennings 2020)
You've documented the age reversal and the late diploma divide, but the broader dealignment thesis makes a specific prediction: the total explained variance of demographic variables (age, gender, education, income, ethnicity) on vote choice should be declining over time as social cleavages weaken. Your NZES data lets you test this directly by comparing the pseudo-R² of your demographic logistic regressions across elections. Your realignment.md shows the coefficients changing, but does the overall predictive power of demographics rise, fall, or stay flat?
8. Strategic Voting under MMP (Cox 1997; Gschwend 2007)
You found no aggregate "squeeze" on minor parties near elections, but that doesn't rule out strategic voting at the individual level. The NZES asks respondents about their most-preferred party vs their actual vote. You could identify the rate of "strategic desertion" — voters who prefer a minor party but vote for a major one — and test whether it increases with the minor party's distance from the 5% threshold in contemporaneous polls. Split-ticket behaviour (different party vote vs electorate vote) is another testable dimension unique to MMP.
