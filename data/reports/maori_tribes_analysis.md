# Māori Voter Tribes: A Subsample Analysis

This report summarizes the k=2 cluster analysis of Māori voters from the NZES 2023 dataset, examining internal diversity within the Māori electorate and how these groupings relate to the broader five-tribe structure.

---

## Overview

While the main analysis identifies five voter tribes across all New Zealanders, a separate k=2 clustering of the Māori subsample (n=695) reveals two distinct political orientations among Māori voters. This analysis uses Māori-specific weights (`mwgt`) to ensure population representativeness.

| Māori Tribe | Size | Left-Right | Top Parties |
|-------------|------|------------|-------------|
| Progressive Māori | 60% | 3.9 (left) | Labour (37%), Te Pāti Māori (14%), Green (13%) |
| Moderate Māori | 40% | 6.0 (centre-right) | Non-vote (32%), National (26%), Labour (14%) |

---

## The Two Māori Tribes

### Progressive Māori (60%)

**Profile**: The majority of Māori voters who hold left-wing views on Treaty issues, redistribution, and social policy.

**Demographics**:
- 57.5% female
- Average age: 45 years
- More politically engaged

**Defining attitudes**:
- Support co-governance arrangements
- Believe Treaty settlements have been appropriate (not gone too far)
- Support Māori representation in decision-making
- Left-wing economic views

**Party support**: Labour (37%), Non-vote (19%), Te Pāti Māori (14%), Green (13%)

**Alignment with main tribes**: Most closely aligned with **Tribe 3: Treaty Advocates**, which is already 37.9% Māori. Both groups share:
- Similar left-right positioning (3.9 vs 3.8)
- Support for Treaty settlements and co-governance
- Female majority
- Younger age profile
- Labour/Green/Te Pāti Māori voting patterns

---

### Moderate Māori (40%)

**Profile**: A substantial minority of Māori voters who hold centre-right views and diverge from progressive Māori consensus on Treaty and identity issues.

**Demographics**:
- 59% male
- Average age: 47 years
- More politically disengaged

**Defining attitudes**:
- Oppose co-governance arrangements
- Believe Treaty settlements have gone too far
- More skeptical of identity-based politics
- Centre-right economic views

**Party support**: Non-vote (32%), National (26%), Labour (14%)

**Alignment with main tribes**: Distributed across centre-right tribes, most closely aligned with:
- **Tribe 4: Skeptical Mainstream** - shares institutional skepticism and swing-voter characteristics
- **Tribe 1: Trusting Traditionalists** - shares centre-right positioning and National support
- **Tribe 5: Economic Individualists** - shares opposition to co-governance

---

## Key Findings

### 1. Māori voters are not monolithic

The 60/40 split challenges any assumption of uniform Māori political preferences. Four in ten Māori voters hold views closer to the centre-right mainstream than to progressive Māori positions.

### 2. The gender divide within Māori politics

Progressive Māori skew female (57.5%), while Moderate Māori skew male (59%). This mirrors the broader gender gap in New Zealand politics but is more pronounced within the Māori electorate.

### 3. Disengagement among Moderate Māori

The high non-vote rate among Moderate Māori (32%) suggests political alienation. Neither major left nor right blocs fully represent their combination of Māori identity with centre-right policy preferences.

### 4. Treaty politics as the dividing line

The sharpest distinction between the two groups is on Treaty-related issues:
- Co-governance support/opposition
- Assessment of whether settlements have gone "too far"
- Views on Māori representation in governance

Economic left-right positioning follows from these identity-related attitudes rather than driving them.

---

## Electoral Implications

1. **Te Pāti Māori's ceiling**: With 40% of Māori voters holding moderate/conservative views, Te Pāti Māori's potential support is structurally limited to roughly 60% of the Māori electorate.

2. **National's Māori opportunity**: The 26% National support among Moderate Māori represents a genuine constituency, not false consciousness. These voters' centre-right views are sincere.

3. **The disengaged middle**: The 32% non-vote rate among Moderate Māori suggests an underserved political market - voters who don't see themselves reflected in either progressive Māori politics or mainstream conservative parties.

4. **Coalition arithmetic**: Labour must balance appeals to both Progressive Māori (who want more on Treaty issues) and Moderate Māori (who want less), while also holding Tribe 2 Engaged Progressives and Tribe 4 Skeptical Mainstream.

---

## Methodological Notes

- **Sample**: Māori by descent or identification (`mmaori = 1`), n=695
- **Weighting**: Māori-specific weight (`mwgt`) used for population estimates
- **Clustering**: k=2 selected based on interpretability and sample size constraints
- **Variables**: Same 40 attitude items as main analysis, subset to Māori respondents

---

## Relationship to Main Five-Tribe Structure

| Māori Tribe | Primary Alignment | Secondary Alignment |
|-------------|-------------------|---------------------|
| Progressive Māori (60%) | Tribe 3: Treaty Advocates | Tribe 2: Engaged Progressives |
| Moderate Māori (40%) | Tribe 4: Skeptical Mainstream | Tribe 1: Trusting Traditionalists |

The k=2 Māori analysis essentially reveals the internal structure within what the main analysis aggregates as "Māori voters." Progressive Māori concentrate in Tribe 3 (which is 37.9% Māori), while Moderate Māori scatter across the centre-right tribes where Māori are typically underrepresented.
