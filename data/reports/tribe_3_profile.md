# Tribe 3: Precariat Left

## Summary

Tribe 3 represents 18.0% of the population (n=358). This group is politically left of centre (mean L-R: 3.8) and predominantly votes for Labour, Green, and Te Pāti Māori. Average age is 42 years with 38% identifying as Māori.

## Who They Are

**In class terms**: Working class to lower-middle class, with significant economic precarity. This tribe has the lowest rate of outright home ownership (11% vs 34%), the highest rate of social housing (11% vs 3%), the most reporting below-average income (18% vs 9%), and the fewest in the top income bracket (6.5% vs 16%).

**Housing tells the story**: Nearly half are renters (private or social housing), and another 19% live with family - likely reflecting both cultural norms and affordability pressures. Only 41% own their home (with or without mortgage), compared to 65% overall.

**Family structure**: The lowest rate of partnership (46% vs 67%). This isn't cultural - it reflects economic constraints. Younger age (42 vs 50) accounts for some of this, but housing insecurity and low income make family formation harder.

**Education**: Not uneducated - university rates are average, and vocational/trade education is above average (20% vs 14%). These are skilled workers - healthcare aides, tradespeople, early childhood educators, social workers - not the unemployed or unskilled.

**Why they're left**: Their politics are material, not merely ideological. They support Treaty settlements and co-governance because these policies benefit their communities directly. They need public housing, public healthcare, and public services — even though they distrust the institutions that deliver them (+0.32 on the distrust scale). Their relationship with the state is complicated: they depend on it and want it to do more, but their experience of it is often one of failure and unresponsiveness.

**The cultural dimension**: With 38% Māori and 10% Pacific, this tribe's politics are shaped by lived experience of colonisation, dispossession, and ongoing structural inequality. Support for Treaty rights isn't abstract - it's about whānau, whenua, and tangible policy outcomes.

## Interpretation

This tribe strongly supports Māori representation in government (-0.94 on opposing "Māori should have more say"), backs co-governance across all domains (-0.80 to -0.88), and believes the Treaty settlement process hasn't gone too far (+0.82). They reject the view that government is run by elites (-0.81) and don't think MPs are out of touch (-0.65).

Their relatively high distrust of institutions (+0.32 on the distrust scale) is matched by high cynicism (-0.64, agreeing with statements like "a person like me has no say" and "government is run by big interests"). Distrust and cynicism go together: these are people who have direct experience of institutional failure — housing, health, justice — and whose cynicism is earned rather than abstract. They strongly agree that MPs are out of touch (-0.65) and that government serves big interests (-0.81).

The contrast with Tribe 2 (Educated Progressives) is instructive: both are left-wing, but Tribe 2's progressivism is ideological (high income, high education, high institutional trust, high efficacy), while Tribe 3's is material (low income, vocational education, low institutional trust, high cynicism). Same votes, different motivations — and different relationships with the institutions they want to change.
