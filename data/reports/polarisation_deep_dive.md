# What Divides New Zealand: A Polarisation Deep Dive

## How the Treaty displaced economics as the primary axis of political division, and why most voters don't know it

---

## The Puzzle

The 2023 NZES analysis produced what looks like a contradiction. Ask voters what issue mattered most to them, and you get a bread-and-butter answer: the economy and cost of living, named by nearly a third of respondents. Treaty issues barely register -- under 2% named anything related to the Treaty or Maori affairs as their most important issue.

But run a principal component analysis on voters' actual attitudes, and the result is unambiguous: the single dimension that captures the most variation in how New Zealanders think about politics is Treaty and co-governance. Not the economy. Not climate. Not institutional trust. Treaty attitudes explain 20.8% of the total variance across 46 political attitude items -- nearly twice as much as the next dimension.

How can the issue that divides voters most be one they barely think about?

---

## Finding 1: The Rise of Treaty Polarisation (2017-2023)

To understand what happened, we ran the same principal component analysis on the 2017 and 2020 NZES datasets. PCA finds the axis along which people are most spread out -- the dimension of maximum disagreement. Whatever loads onto PC1 is, in a statistical sense, what most divides the electorate.

### 2017: A Classic Economic Left-Right Axis

In 2017, PC1 (19.6% of variance) was a textbook economic left-right dimension. The strongest loadings were government spending items: spend more on the environment (+0.260), welfare (+0.255), housing (+0.253), reduce income differences (+0.249). On the opposite pole: lower welfare benefits to encourage self-reliance (-0.235), unions have too much power (-0.193).

The Treaty was present but subordinate. "Remove the Treaty from law" loaded at -0.224 -- the sixth-strongest loading, embedded within a broader left-right package rather than defining its own dimension. Treaty attitudes were part of the left-right axis, not the core of it.

### 2020: The Treaty Begins to Rise

By 2020, PC1 (18.4% of variance) was still fundamentally economic, but Treaty items had climbed. The top loadings were stronger climate policy (+0.258), reduce income differences (+0.254), and methane emissions (+0.242) -- but "Maori should have more say in all government decisions" had risen to the fourth-strongest loading (+0.239), and "Remove the Treaty from law" was now the top negative loading (-0.231).

Between 2017 and 2020, Treaty attitudes moved from embedded-in-economics to co-equal-with-economics as an organising dimension. The two items that track Treaty attitudes -- "Maori more say" and "remove Treaty from law" -- both strengthened their loadings on the primary axis while spending items weakened.

### 2023: Treaty Takes Over

By 2023, the transformation was complete. PC1 (20.8% of variance) was entirely Treaty and co-governance:

| Loading | Variable |
|---------|----------|
| +0.250 | Maori should have more say in all government decisions |
| +0.249 | Co-governance in appointment of public entity boards |
| +0.246 | Co-governance in management of natural resources |
| +0.243 | Co-governance in regional planning |
| +0.210 | Co-governance in establishment of new government agencies |
| -0.244 | Treaty settlements have gone too far |
| -0.243 | Remove Treaty references from law |
| -0.210 | Policies to increase women in politics have gone too far |

All five of the top positive loadings and both of the top negative loadings are Treaty/Maori items. Economic redistribution -- which dominated PC1 in 2017 -- has been displaced entirely.

Between these elections, the He Puapua report leaked (2021), Three Waters and co-governance dominated political debate (2022-23), and co-governance became the defining wedge issue of the 2023 campaign. The 2023 NZES added four new co-governance questions (C9a-d) that captured this shift, but even the older Treaty questions that were asked identically across all three surveys show the trajectory clearly.

### The Second Dimension Shifted Too

PC2 tells its own story:

- **2017** (8.7%): Social authoritarianism -- more police spending, death penalty, women should stay home
- **2020** (12.1%): Political cynicism -- MPs out of touch, politicians don't care, government run by big interests
- **2023** (11.5%): Institutional trust vs scepticism -- trust in parliament and government vs "don't care what I think"

The crystallisation of political disillusionment as a distinct dimension between 2017 and 2020 foreshadowed the trust-vs-scepticism divide visible in the 2023 clustering. The Disaffected Conservatives (17%) anchor the distrustful end of this dimension while the Moderate Mainstream (26%) anchor the trusting end. The emergence of institutional trust/distrust as a distinct statistical dimension preceded its role in differentiating the political tribes.

## Finding 2: The Salience-Polarisation Disconnect

When voters were asked "What was the single most important issue for you in the 2023 election?", their answers (coded from open-ended text) were:

| Issue | % of voters (weighted) |
|-------|----------------------|
| Economy (general) | 17.2% |
| Cost of living / inflation | 14.5% |
| Health | 7.3% |
| Poverty / inequality | 6.3% |
| Environment | 5.1% |
| Tax | 4.9% |
| Law and order | 4.2% |
| Identity / fairness | 4.2% |
| Housing | 3.9% |
| ... | ... |
| **Maori / Treaty** | **1.7%** |

Economy and cost of living combined account for 31.7% of responses. Treaty and Maori issues: 1.7%.

This is not a contradiction with the PCA finding. It reflects a distinction between **salience** (what people think about) and **polarisation** (what divides them). Cost of living is highly salient -- everyone worries about it -- but it doesn't divide people into opposing camps because virtually everyone agrees prices are too high. Treaty and co-governance are low-salience -- most voters don't spontaneously name them -- but they sharply divide those who hold opinions on them.

The implication is that Treaty/co-governance polarisation may be more consequential than voters themselves realise. It structures the entire attitude space: someone's position on co-governance correlates with their views on redistribution, climate, unions, gender equity, and more. It organises political identities even when voters think they're voting on the cost of groceries.

## Finding 3: Eta-Squared Tells a Different Story (And That's OK)

The PCA finding sits alongside a complementary but different result from the k=5 clustering analysis. When you form five voter tribes via k-means clustering and ask which attitude variable differs most between those tribes (via eta-squared), the answer in all three years is economic redistribution:

| Year | Most polarising by eta-squared | eta-squared |
|------|-------------------------------|-------------|
| 2017 | Income differences too large | 0.419 |
| 2020 | Government responsible for housing | 0.416 |
| 2023 | Government should reduce income differences | 0.695 |

Treaty items consistently rank mid-table on eta-squared: around 0.21-0.30, placing them roughly 6th-14th depending on year and question.

These measures answer different questions. PCA asks: "What single dimension captures the most total attitude variation?" Eta-squared asks: "Which variable differs most between these five specific groups?" Redistribution wins on eta-squared because the five tribes are arranged primarily along an economic axis; within that arrangement, redistribution attitudes diverge sharply. Treaty/co-governance wins on PCA because it connects to more other attitudes -- it's the broadest organising principle, not necessarily the sharpest single dividing line.

If you could ask New Zealanders one question to predict their overall political worldview, it should be about Treaty/co-governance. If you could ask one question to identify which of the five tribes they belong to, it should be about redistribution.

## Finding 4: The Biggest Demographic Gap Isn't Age x Gender -- It's Maori

Previous analysis identified the age-by-gender interaction as the widest demographic gap: older men (mean 5.88 on the 0-10 left-right scale) versus younger women (4.61), a 1.27-point spread. This is correct for that specific two-way interaction. But it's not the widest gap in the data.

### Single Demographics (2023, weighted)

| Dimension | Gap (L-R points) | Most right | Most left |
|-----------|------------------|-----------|----------|
| Age | 1.04 | 40+ | Under 40 |
| Education | 0.92 | No university | University |
| Home ownership | 0.84 | Owner | Renter |
| Maori | 0.80 | Non-Maori | Maori |
| Gender | 0.33 | Male | Female |
| Urban/rural | 0.21 | Rural | Urban |

### Two-Way Interactions

| Interaction | Gap | Most right | Most left |
|-------------|-----|-----------|----------|
| Education x Maori | 1.85 | Non-Maori, no university | Maori, university-educated |
| Age x Education | 1.64 | 40+, no university | Under 40, university |
| Age x Maori | 1.60 | 40+, non-Maori | Under 40, Maori |
| Education x Home | 1.51 | No-uni owner | University renter |
| Age x Home | 1.36 | 40+ owner | Under 40 renter |
| Gender x Education | 1.35 | No-uni female | University female |
| Gender x Maori | 1.29 | Male, non-Maori | Female, Maori |
| Age x Gender | 1.27 | Male, 40+ | Female, under 40 |

Age x gender (1.27) is actually only the eighth-widest two-way interaction. Education x Maori (1.85) is the widest. Maori identity appears in four of the top five two-way interactions.

### Three-Way Interactions

| Interaction | Gap | Most right | Most left |
|-------------|-----|-----------|----------|
| **Age x Gender x Maori** | **2.59** | **40+ male, non-Maori** | **Under 40 female, Maori** |
| Age x Education x Maori | 2.48 | 40+ no-uni, non-Maori | Under 40 uni, Maori |
| Age x Education x Home | 2.45 | 40+ no-uni, owner | Under 40 uni, renter |
| Gender x Education x Maori | 2.11 | No-uni female, non-Maori | Uni female, Maori |
| Age x Gender x Urban | 1.96 | 40+ male, urban | Under 40 female, rural |
| Age x Gender x Education | 1.94 | 40+ male, no university | Under 40 female, university |
| Age x Gender x Home | 1.71 | 40+ male, owner | Under 40 female, renter |

The widest gap in New Zealand politics is **2.59 points** between older non-Maori men and younger Maori women. That's more than a quarter of the entire 0-10 left-right scale. Age x gender x education, the combination that might seem most natural given international literature on education polarisation, ranks only sixth at 1.94 points.

Maori identity is the most powerful demographic multiplier. It appears in the top four three-way interactions. Adding Maori to any two-way combination widens the gap by 0.3-0.6 points -- more than any other single demographic dimension adds.

### The Full Eight-Way Split: Age x Gender x Education

For reference, the detailed breakdown of the age-gender-education interaction:

| Group | Mean L-R | n |
|-------|---------|---|
| 40+ male, no university | 6.00 | 417 |
| 40+ female, no university | 5.89 | 401 |
| 40+ male, university | 5.58 | 228 |
| Under 40 female, no university | 5.11 | 77 |
| Under 40 male, university | 4.76 | 76 |
| Under 40 male, no university | 4.71 | 76 |
| 40+ female, university | 4.55 | 293 |
| Under 40 female, university | 4.05 | 150 |

Education matters more within the 40+ group (0.42-point gap for men, 1.34 for women) than the under-40 group (0.05 for men, 1.06 for women). Among older women, university education shifts attitudes leftward by over a full point. Among younger men, it barely registers. The interaction effects are not symmetric.

### A Caveat on Small Cells

The Maori three-way interactions involve small subgroups. Under-40 Maori women number in the low dozens, meaning these estimates carry substantial sampling error. The pattern is consistent across all combinations involving Maori identity, which increases confidence, but the specific gap sizes should be treated as approximate.

## Putting It Together

Three findings from this analysis update the story told in the main report:

1. **Treaty/co-governance has risen from a component of economic left-right politics (2017) to the primary organising dimension of the entire attitude space (2023).** This happened gradually through 2020 and then accelerated during the co-governance debate of 2021-23. The shift is visible even in questions asked identically across all three surveys.

2. **The most polarising issue and the most important issue to voters are different things.** Cost of living dominates voter salience (32%); Treaty/co-governance dominates attitude structure (PC1 at 20.8%). Voters are deeply divided on Treaty issues without necessarily thinking of them as their primary concern. This suggests Treaty polarisation may operate as a background structuring force -- shaping political identities and party allegiances even when voters believe they're responding to economic conditions.

3. **The widest demographic gap in New Zealand politics involves Maori identity, not education.** Age x gender x Maori (2.59 points) exceeds age x gender x education (1.94 points) by a third. Maori identity is the most powerful demographic amplifier: it widens every two-way gap it's added to. The Maori-non-Maori left-right gap doubled between 2017 and 2023, driven by Maori moving left while non-Maori stayed put. Combined with age and gender, this creates the widest attitudinal chasm in the electorate.

The common thread: Treaty and Maori politics is more structurally important to New Zealand's political landscape than voters, commentators, or possibly even politicians fully appreciate. It organises attitudes, amplifies demographic divides, and has displaced economics as the primary axis of political contestation -- all while remaining a low-salience issue that most voters don't spontaneously name as what matters to them.

## Methodological Notes

**Data**: New Zealand Election Study 2017 (n=3,455), 2020 (n=3,730), 2023 (n=1,989). All attitude analyses weighted by survey weights (weight5, lfinwt, vwgt respectively). Clustering unweighted.

**PCA**: Principal component analysis on standardised attitude variables after median imputation of missing values. Variable sets differ by year (29 in 2017, 32 in 2020, 46 in 2023) because different surveys included different questions. Co-governance questions (C9a-d) are unique to 2023. The older Treaty questions ("remove Treaty from law", "Maori should have more say") were asked in all three years and show rising PC1 loadings even without the new co-governance items.

**Eta-squared**: Computed as SS(between clusters) / SS(total) for each attitude variable against k=5 k-means cluster assignments (random_state=42, n_init=50). Different variable sets produce different cluster structures, so absolute eta-squared values are not directly comparable across years; within-year rankings are the meaningful comparison.

**Demographic gaps**: Weighted means on the 0-10 left-right self-placement scale (B6/B8/rselflr). Binary age split at 40. Binary education: university degree vs no university degree. Gap = max group mean minus min group mean across all cells in the interaction. Small-cell estimates (n < 50) should be treated with caution.

**Most important issue**: Open-ended question C2x ("What was the single most important issue for you in the 2023 election?"), coded into categories by the NZES team (miicat variable). Weighted by vwgt. Multiple binary flags (minflation, mtiriti, etc.) also available for responses mentioning multiple issues.
