# Extended NZES Analysis Summary

This report extends the core five-tribe analysis with temporal comparisons (1990--2023), attitude drift analysis, within-tribe vote variation, demographic polarisation patterns, interaction effects, and question-level consensus analysis.

## Executive Summary

**Key findings:**

1. **Tribe Stability**: Political tribes show remarkable stability across 2017-2023, with cluster correlations exceeding 0.99 between year pairs
2. **Age x Gender is the largest divide**: The gap between older men (L-R 5.88) and younger women (4.61) is 1.27 points and widening -- larger than any single demographic dimension alone
3. **Education Polarisation**: University graduates are consistently more left-leaning (0.7-1.0 points on 0-10 scale), with the gap widening to 0.95 in 2023. The relationship is a gradient, not a threshold: postgraduates are distinctly more left than bachelor holders
4. **Gender Gap**: Men are consistently more right-leaning than women, with the gap peaking in 2020 (0.59 points) before moderating in 2023 (0.33 points)
5. **Age Divide**: Young voters (under 30) are significantly more left than older voters (60+), with a persistent gap of 1.0-1.5 points
6. **Housing Tenure Cleavage**: Home owners are consistently more right-leaning than renters, with the gap compounded by age -- older owners sit furthest right, young renters furthest left
7. **Maori Divergence Widening**: The Maori-non-Maori left-right gap more than doubled from 0.34 to 0.80 points between 2017 and 2023
8. **Consensus on democratic values**: All five tribes agree on institutional cynicism, social media distrust, and the importance of judicial checks on government -- these topics have near-zero between-tribe variance (eta² < 0.02). By contrast, income redistribution dominates the divide (eta² = 0.70)
9. **1990 tribes are recognisable**: A five-cluster analysis of the 1990 NZES (52 attitude variables, n=1,893) produces groups that map onto the 2023 tribes, with one key difference: in 1990, a large centre-left working class cluster (25%) was economically progressive but opposed to Maori political voice -- a combination that has no clear 2023 equivalent

## 1. Temporal Tribe Analysis (2017-2023)

### Data and Methods

Cross-year analysis used harmonised attitude variables available across all three NZES waves:
- Left-right self-placement (0-10 scale)
- Support for income redistribution (1-5 agree scale)
- Perception of income inequality (1-5 agree scale)

Sample sizes: 2017 (n=3,455), 2020 (n=3,730), 2023 (n=1,989)

### Cluster Stability

**Finding: Political tribes are highly stable across election cycles.**

The k=5 clustering solution shows near-perfect correlation between matched clusters across years:

| Year Pair | Mean Correlation |
|-----------|------------------|
| 2017-2020 | 0.9997 |
| 2020-2023 | 0.9998 |

This suggests that the underlying attitudinal structure of the New Zealand electorate has remained fundamentally stable over the past three elections, despite significant political events (COVID-19, change of government in 2023).

### Cluster Size Changes

While the structure remained stable, relative tribe sizes shifted:

| Cluster | 2017 | 2020 | 2023 | Trend |
|---------|------|------|------|-------|
| 0 | 17% | 25% | 28% | Growing |
| 1 | 22% | 30% | 18% | Volatile |
| 2 | 13% | 14% | 21% | Growing |
| 3 | 32% | 17% | 19% | Declining |
| 4 | 16% | 14% | 14% | Stable |

**Interpretation**: The COVID-19 pandemic (2020 election) appears to have temporarily shifted voter distributions, with 2023 showing partial reversion to pre-pandemic patterns.

## 2. Attitude Drift Analysis

### Left-Right Self-Placement Trends

The New Zealand electorate shows modest but measurable attitude drift by demographic group:

#### By Education

| Year | University | No University | Gap |
|------|------------|---------------|-----|
| 2017 | 4.75 | 5.60 | -0.85 |
| 2020 | 4.81 | 5.54 | -0.72 |
| 2023 | 4.68 | 5.63 | -0.95 |

**Finding**: The education gap narrowed slightly in 2020 (possibly a COVID-19 rally effect) but widened to its largest level in 2023. This aligns with international patterns of educational polarisation.

#### By Gender

| Year | Male | Female | Gap |
|------|------|--------|-----|
| 2017 | 5.46 | 5.23 | +0.22 |
| 2020 | 5.72 | 5.14 | +0.59 |
| 2023 | 5.51 | 5.18 | +0.33 |

**Finding**: The gender gap spiked in 2020 (men moved right, women stayed put) but moderated in 2023. This may reflect differential responses to the Ardern government's COVID-19 approach.

#### By Age

| Year | Under 30 | 60+ | Gap |
|------|----------|-----|-----|
| 2017 | 4.49 | 5.93 | +1.45 |
| 2020 | 4.83 | 5.79 | +0.96 |
| 2023 | 4.49 | 5.85 | +1.36 |

**Finding**: The age gap compressed in 2020 (young voters moved slightly right) but returned to near-2017 levels in 2023. Young voters remain significantly more left-leaning.

## 3. Within-Tribe Vote Variation

Not all members of a tribe vote the same way. Key within-tribe variations from the 2023 analysis:

### Educated Progressives
- Primary vote: Labour (39%)
- Secondary: Greens (25%)
- **Within-tribe divide**: Economic progressives vs environmental progressives

### Precariat Left
- Primary vote: Labour (37%)
- Secondary: Te Pati Maori (15%)
- **Within-tribe divide**: Labour-leaning vs TPM-leaning, often along urban/rural and age lines

### Middle New Zealand
- Primary vote: National (39%)
- Secondary: Labour (23%)
- **Within-tribe divide**: This is the classic swing voter tribe. Their vote follows government performance.

### Establishment Right
- Primary vote: National (64%)
- Secondary: ACT (17%)
- **Within-tribe divide**: Pragmatic conservatives (National) vs libertarian-leaning (ACT)

### Alienated Conservatives
- Primary vote: National (38%)
- Secondary: Non-vote (20%), Labour (11%)
- **Within-tribe divide**: Immigration status and religiosity affect party choice. Highest non-vote rate suggests some have disengaged from electoral politics entirely.

## 4. Demographic Polarisation Analysis

### Education Divide

**Finding: The education divide is real but modest in New Zealand, and is a gradient rather than a threshold.**

- University graduates are 0.7-1.0 points more left on the 0-10 scale
- The gap appears to be widening (largest in 2023)
- NZ pattern is similar to but weaker than US/UK education polarisation

| Attitude | Uni - Non-Uni Gap (2023) |
|----------|-------------------------|
| Left-right | -0.95 (Uni more left) |
| Redistribution | Uni more supportive |
| Income inequality | Uni more concerned |

Fine-grained education analysis (2017-2023) using a 4-level classification reveals that the relationship between education and political attitudes is a gradient, not a sharp threshold at university level. Each step from no qualification through trade/diploma, bachelor, and postgraduate is associated with a leftward shift of roughly equal size. Postgraduates -- including academics, policy professionals, and senior public servants -- are meaningfully more left than bachelor-degree holders on both left-right self-placement and redistribution attitudes.

### Gender Gap

**Finding: Men are more right-leaning, but the gap is smaller than in some countries.**

- The gender gap peaked during COVID-19 (2020: 0.59 points)
- The gap moderated in 2023 (0.33 points)
- Young women are substantially more left than young men (intersectional effect)

### Age Polarisation

**Finding: The age divide is the largest demographic gap in NZ politics.**

- Gap between youngest (under 30) and oldest (60+) cohorts: 1.4 points
- This is larger than the education gap (0.95) or gender gap (0.33)
- The gap is primarily driven by young voters being left-leaning, not older voters becoming more conservative

### Housing Tenure

**Finding: Home ownership is a significant political cleavage that compounds with age.**

- Home owners are consistently more right-leaning than renters across all years with data (1990, 2002, 2011, 2023)
- Owners are less supportive of government redistribution, consistent with material self-interest
- The age-ownership interaction creates four politically distinct groups: older owners (furthest right), older renters (centre-right), younger owners (centre), younger renters (furthest left)
- As home ownership rates decline among young New Zealanders, the renter population grows and becomes more politically distinctive

### Maori Attitude Drift

**Finding: The Maori-non-Maori attitudinal gap is widening rapidly.**

| Year | Maori L-R | Non-Maori L-R | Gap |
|------|-----------|---------------|-----|
| 2017 | 5.05 | 5.39 | -0.34 |
| 2020 | 5.16 | 5.47 | -0.31 |
| 2023 | 4.65 | 5.45 | -0.80 |

The left-right gap more than doubled between 2017 and 2023. Maori moved substantially leftward in 2023 (from 5.16 to 4.65), while non-Maori attitudes remained stable. Labour's Maori vote share fell dramatically from 56.7% to 27.7% over the same period, with the emergence of Te Pati Maori (9.8% among Maori in 2023) fracturing the Maori centre-left vote.

### Geographic Patterns

A 4-level urban-rural classification (Rural, Small Town, Suburban, Urban City; available 2020-2023) reveals a gradient from right (Rural) to left (Urban City), though the total span is smaller than education or age effects. The biggest attitudinal jump is between Small Town and Suburban, suggesting a partial threshold at the boundary between towns and metropolitan areas.

Party vote varies across the gradient: Rural areas are dominated by National and NZ First; Urban City areas show stronger Labour and Green support; Suburban areas are competitive terrain.

## 5. Demographic Interaction Effects

Single-variable analyses understate the true extent of polarisation. The largest divides emerge when demographic dimensions are crossed.

### Age x Gender (the widest gap)

| Year | 40+ Male | Under 40 Female | Gap |
|------|----------|-----------------|-----|
| 2017 | 5.69 | 4.50 | 1.19 |
| 2020 | 5.92 | 4.72 | 1.19 |
| 2023 | 5.88 | 4.61 | 1.27 |

The age-gender interaction produces the largest and most consistent attitudinal gap in New Zealand politics -- 1.27 points in 2023 and widening. This is larger than the age gap alone (1.36), the gender gap alone (0.33), or the education gap (0.95).

### Age x Education

| Year | 40+ No Uni | Under 40 Uni | Gap |
|------|-----------|--------------|-----|
| 2017 | 5.61 | 4.74 | 0.87 |
| 2020 | 5.18 | 4.29 | 0.89 |
| 2023 | 5.69 | 4.66 | 1.03 |

The age-education interaction gap is widening (0.87 to 1.03 points). Older non-graduates and younger graduates are pulling apart.

### Education x Gender

The gender gap is larger among non-university respondents. University education partially equalises male and female political attitudes, suggesting that higher education may dampen gendered political socialisation.

## 6. Question-Level Consensus and Polarisation (2023)

Not all questions divide New Zealanders equally. An eta-squared analysis of the 46 clustering variables -- measuring how much of each question's variance is explained by tribe membership -- reveals that democratic values and institutional cynicism unite the electorate, while economic redistribution and Treaty politics divide it.

### Where the Tribes Agree (lowest eta²)

| eta² | Variable | Question | Consensus position |
|------|----------|----------|-------------------|
| 0.005 | A11g | Trust in social media | All tribes distrust (~3.3/4) |
| 0.009 | A11c | Trust in judges and courts | All mildly trust (~2.0/4) |
| 0.009 | A9c | Country run better by referendums | Mild agreement across all |
| 0.010 | G12g | Politicians don't care about people | All mildly agree (~2.7/5) |
| 0.011 | A11b | Trust in government | All moderate (~2.3/4) |
| 0.012 | A8c | Courts should check government power | All agree (~1.9/5) |
| 0.013 | G12a | People would take advantage of others | Mild agreement across all |
| 0.015 | A11a | Trust in parliament | All moderate (~2.3/4) |
| 0.017 | A11e | Trust in political parties generally | All mildly distrust (~2.7/4) |
| 0.017 | G12d | MPs are out of touch | All mildly agree (~2.5/5) |

**Finding: All five tribes agree that social media is untrustworthy, courts should check government power, politicians are out of touch, and people are generally self-interested.** These democratic values and cynicism items have near-zero between-tribe variance (eta² < 0.02), meaning tribe membership explains virtually none of the variation in responses.

The consensus items share a common theme: they reflect judgements about the political *system* rather than about specific *policies*. New Zealanders across the political spectrum share a common view of institutional trustworthiness -- they just disagree about what to do about it.

### Where the Tribes Diverge (highest eta²)

| eta² | Variable | Question |
|------|----------|----------|
| 0.695 | C6a | Government should reduce income differences |
| 0.688 | C12c | Income differences in NZ are too large |
| 0.249 | C9a | Co-governance in conservation management |
| 0.240 | C9b | Co-governance in tribunal appointments |
| 0.239 | A8d | Policies to increase women's representation |
| 0.227 | C12i | Maori should have more say in government |
| 0.224 | C12a | Trade unions are necessary to protect workers |
| 0.221 | C11 | Treaty settlement process: too far or not enough? |
| 0.217 | C12h | Capital gains should not be taxed |
| 0.214 | C12g | Stronger policies needed to reduce carbon emissions |

**Finding: Income redistribution is the dominant dividing line.** The two redistribution items (C6a and C12c) have eta-squared values above 0.68 -- roughly 70x more divisive than social media trust. Treaty/co-governance items form the second cluster of division (eta² = 0.22--0.25), followed by women's representation and union policy.

### The Scale of Difference

Across 46 clustering variables:
- **Median eta²**: 0.124
- **Range**: 0.005 (social media trust) to 0.695 (income redistribution)
- **Top 2 variables alone** explain more between-tribe variance than the bottom 30 combined

This concentration has an important implication: if income redistribution attitudes were removed from the clustering, the tribe structure would likely change substantially. The tribes are primarily defined by where people sit on the redistribution question, with Treaty/co-governance as a secondary axis.

## 7. Five-Tribe Analysis of 1990 (n=1,893)

The 1990 NZES contains 52 attitude and policy variables covering constitutional reform, social issues, Treaty/Maori policy, economic redistribution, government spending, state ownership, labour relations, and defence. This is a richer set than the harmonised cross-year variables used elsewhere in this report, enabling a full k=5 cluster analysis comparable to the 2023 five-tribe model.

### Data and Methods

- **Dataset**: NZES 1990, n=2,102 (1,893 retained after excluding respondents with >50% missing on clustering variables)
- **Variables**: 52 attitude items across 10 policy domains (see appendix below)
- **Missing values**: 0="Can't say" recoded to NaN for applicable variables; remaining missing imputed with median
- **Method**: K-means (k=5), standardised variables, best of 50 random initialisations
- **Silhouette score**: 0.034 (comparable to 2023's 0.010 on 46 variables -- low scores are typical for high-dimensional survey data)
- **No survey weights applied** (none available in 1990 dataset)

### The Five 1990 Tribes

#### Tribe 0: Social Conservatives (n=462, 24.4%)

The largest right-leaning cluster. Older (mean age 51), low education (5% university degree), high home ownership (81%). Strongly aligned with National (79% voted National in 1990). Defined by:

- Right on left-right scale (z=+0.42) and anti-redistribution (+0.15)
- Strongly pro-nuclear ship visits (z=-0.70 on opposition scale) and pro-US defence ties
- Socially conservative: opposed to women's equality and homosexual rights reforms
- Anti-Treaty: opposed to Maori say in government and Treaty as law
- Anti-union: supported voluntary unionism, sceptical of union necessity
- 59% identified as National supporters, only 7% Labour

#### Tribe 1: Moderate Centrists (n=404, 21.3%)

The classic swing voters. Youngest cluster (mean age 41), most female (59%), moderate education (14% university). No dominant party attachment (44% identified with no party). Mixed voting: 37% Labour, 33% National, 12% Green. Defined by:

- Near the centre on every dimension: left-right (z=-0.12), redistribution (+0.02)
- Slightly free-market leaning (z=-0.23 on intervention scale)
- Moderate on Treaty/Maori issues
- More socially liberal: mildly pro women's equality and homosexual rights
- Pro nuclear-free policy (z=+0.30)
- The 1990 equivalent of the 2023 Middle New Zealand

#### Tribe 2: Economic Right (n=292, 15.4%)

The free-market elite. Male-dominated (68%), highest education (19% university), highest income, high home ownership (80%). Voted 66% National. Defined by:

- Most right-wing on left-right scale (z=+0.65), most anti-redistribution (z=+0.83)
- Most pro-free-market (z=-0.45 on intervention scale), most pro-private health (z=+0.73)
- Most opposed to government providing jobs (+0.80) and spending on low incomes (+0.81)
- Strongly anti-Maori say in government (+0.62)
- Anti-union: supported voluntary unionism, sceptical of union necessity
- 48% identified as National supporters; subjectively middle class (54%)

#### Tribe 3: Left Progressives (n=257, 13.6%)

The smallest and most distinctive cluster. Youngest (mean age 41), most female (61%), highest education (tied at 19% university), lowest home ownership (51%). Voted 48% Labour, 12% Green, 8% NewLabour. Defined by:

- Most left on left-right scale (z=-0.65), most pro-redistribution (z=-0.56)
- **Strongly pro-Treaty**: z=+1.55 on Treaty as law, z=-1.20 on opposing Maori say in government (the strongest single signal of any cluster on any variable)
- Most pro women's equality (z=+0.62), most pro homosexual rights
- Most pro nuclear-free policy (z=+0.55), most pro-environment (z=-0.36)
- Pro-union: wanted compulsory unionism, believed unions were necessary
- Pro public health (z=-0.44), pro government role in jobs and welfare
- 43% Labour attachment, 9% National; the most ideologically coherent cluster

#### Tribe 4: Centre-Left Working Class (n=478, 25.3%)

The largest cluster. Moderate age (45), balanced gender, very low education (3.4% university), moderate home ownership (59%). Voted 40% Labour, 26% National, 9% Green, 7% NewLabour. Defined by:

- Left-leaning on economics: pro-redistribution (z=-0.37), pro-public health (z=-0.39), pro-government jobs (-0.39)
- **Strongly pro-union**: wanted compulsory unionism (z=+0.51), believed unions were necessary (-0.37)
- Pro nuclear-free policy and moderately pro-intervention
- **But anti-Maori say** in government (z=+0.15), Treaty gone too far (z=-0.30)
- Neutral on social issues: neither especially pro nor anti women's equality or homosexual rights
- Most working-class self-identification (38%); 35% Labour attachment
- Economically left but socially and culturally conservative on identity issues

### What Changed Between 1990 and 2023

**The progressive-Treaty realignment.** In 1990, strong pro-Treaty views were confined to a single small cluster (Left Progressives, 14%). By 2023, the pro-Treaty constituency has grown and split into two large groups: Educated Progressives (21%, educated professionals) and Precariat Left (18%, working-class Maori/Pacific). The progressive coalition broadened and diversified.

**The disappearance of the "left but anti-Maori" voter.** The Centre-Left Working Class of 1990 -- a quarter of the electorate -- combined economic progressivism with opposition to Maori political voice. This position has no clear 2023 equivalent. The economic left and the identity-politics left have converged: by 2023, pro-redistribution attitudes are strongly correlated with pro-Treaty attitudes (PC1 of the attitude space loads on both). The 1990 voters who were economically left but culturally conservative on Maori issues appear to have been absorbed into either the Middle New Zealand (if they moved right) or Precariat Left (if they moved left on identity).

**The growth of the educated left.** The Left Progressives of 1990 (14%) were a small, highly ideological group. Their 2023 successor -- the Educated Progressives -- is larger (21%) and broader, reflecting the expansion of university education and the increasing correlation between education and left-wing attitudes.

**Structural stability despite surface change.** The broad architecture -- a right-wing economic elite, a conservative social base, a centrist swing group, and a left-progressive intelligentsia -- is recognisable across 33 years. What changed is the composition and relative size of these groups, and the alignment between economic and identity dimensions.

## 8. Implications

### For Electoral Politics

1. **The median voter**: The Middle New Zealand tribe (26%) determines elections. Their high institutional trust but low personal efficacy makes them responsive to mainstream political messaging from either major party. Soft clustering confirms this: Middle New Zealand is the most "porous" tribe, sharing boundary respondents with all other tribes.

2. **Demographic targeting**: The interaction of age and gender produces the widest attitudinal gap (1.27 points). Parties gain more from mobilising their demographic intersections (e.g., young women for the left, older men for the right) than from targeting any single dimension.

3. **Stability amid change**: Despite major events (COVID-19, government change), the underlying tribal structure of NZ politics has remained stable. Voters shift between tribes more slowly than they shift between parties. The 1990 analysis extends this finding back 33 years: the broad architecture of right-wing elite, conservative base, centrist swing, and progressive left is recognisable across a third of a century.

4. **Generational replacement, not conversion**: Birth cohort analysis shows 1980s and 1990s cohorts are not shifting rightward as they age. If cohort effects persist, generational replacement will gradually move the electorate leftward.

5. **Common ground exists**: The consensus analysis shows that democratic values -- support for judicial checks on government, distrust of social media, cynicism about politicians -- unite the electorate across all five tribes. These shared attitudes could provide a foundation for cross-tribal political messaging.

### For Understanding NZ Society

1. **Class still matters**: The five tribes map roughly onto class positions, from working-class Precariat Left to upper-class Establishment Right. Housing tenure -- owner vs renter -- is a significant political cleavage that reinforces this class dimension.

2. **Multiple left-wing constituencies**: The left coalition includes both educated professionals (Educated Progressives) and working-class/Maori voters (Precariat Left). These groups share voting patterns but have different motivations. The 1990 analysis suggests this split is relatively recent: in 1990, the pro-Treaty left was a single small cluster.

3. **Trust is polarised**: The Alienated Conservatives show the lowest institutional trust of any tribe (+0.62 on the distrust scale), while the Middle New Zealand show the highest trust (-0.39). This cross-cuts the left-right divide — the most trusting tribe is centre-right, while the most distrustful tribes span both left (Precariat Left, +0.32) and right (Alienated Conservatives, +0.62). Affective polarisation (trust in in-party vs out-party government) is symmetric between Labour and National voters, at approximately 1.14 points on a 4-point scale. Precariat Left show the highest affective polarisation (1.77 among Labour voters), while Middle New Zealand voters show the lowest (~0.75 on both sides), consistent with their non-ideological orientation.

4. **Treaty politics is the primary axis**: PCA of the full attitude space shows Treaty/co-governance attitudes as the first principal component (20.8% of variance), ahead of institutional trust (11.5%). The correlation network confirms Treaty items are among the most broadly connected in the attitude space. The 1990 comparison puts this in historical context: Treaty attitudes have moved from a peripheral issue (small activist cluster) to the defining political axis.

5. **Maori political divergence**: The widening gap between Maori and non-Maori attitudes, combined with Labour's collapsing Maori vote share and the rise of Te Pati Maori, suggests a structural realignment of Maori political representation.

6. **The economic-identity realignment**: In 1990, a quarter of the electorate combined pro-redistribution economics with opposition to Maori political voice. This "left but anti-Maori" position has largely disappeared by 2023, replaced by a tighter alignment between economic progressivism and support for Treaty/co-governance. Income redistribution and Treaty attitudes now load on the same principal component -- they were once separate dimensions.

## Technical Notes

### Data Sources
- NZES 1990 (n=2,102)
- NZES 2017 (n=3,455)
- NZES 2020 (n=3,730)
- NZES 2023 (n=1,989)

Note: 2002 and 2011 data were unavailable for this run; analyses using harmonised panels loaded available years only.

### Weighting
- Clustering: Unweighted (to find natural groupings)
- Descriptive statistics: Weighted (`vwgt` for 2023, year-specific weights for earlier years)

### Limitations
- Cross-sectional data -- cannot track individual voters over time
- Limited common variables across all years constrains temporal analysis
- 2023 sample is smaller, affecting precision for subgroup analyses
- Housing tenure data available only for 1990, 2002, 2011, 2023 (not 2017, 2020)
- Fine-grained education (4-level) only available from 2017; 2017 cannot distinguish bachelor from postgraduate
- Maori identification unavailable in 1990
- 1990 cluster analysis uses median imputation (simpler than MICE used for 2023) and no survey weights (none available)
- The 1990-2023 comparison is suggestive, not a direct match: different clustering variables, different imputation methods, and different sample compositions mean the clusters cannot be formally aligned
