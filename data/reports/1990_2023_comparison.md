# 1990 vs 2023: Structural Change in New Zealand's Voter Tribes

This report compares five-tribe cluster analyses of the 1990 and 2023 New Zealand Election Studies, tracing how the political tribe structure of the electorate has changed over 33 years and two electoral systems.

---

## Executive Summary

1. **The cross-class progressive coalition fractured.** In 1990, university-educated Pakeha liberals and working-class Maori sat in the same political tribe. By 2023, they occupy two distinct tribes -- the Educated Progressives (upper-middle class) and the Precariat Left (working class, disproportionately Maori/Pacific).

2. **The "left but anti-Maori" voter bloc disappeared.** The Traditional Working Class (25% in 1990) combined left economic views with opposition to Maori political voice. No 2023 tribe replicates this combination. Pro-Treaty sentiment grew from one small cluster (14%) to two large ones (39% combined).

3. **The petite bourgeoisie became the alienated lower-middle class.** The 1990 Social Conservatives were asset-rich property owners with low formal education. Their 2023 successors, the Alienated Conservatives, are lower-middle class, institutionally distrustful, and far more likely to not vote (20% vs 5%).

4. **Non-voting rose across the board, but especially at the bottom.** The Precariat Left (19% non-vote) and Alienated Conservatives (20%) now have non-voting rates three to four times higher than their 1990 equivalents.

5. **Maori voters were a distinct political force even in 1990** -- but only visible with demographic correction. Census-weighted clustering produces a majority-Maori cluster; without weighting, Maori are subsumed into the broader progressive tribe due to severe undersampling (5.5% vs 12.5% Census).

## 1. The Five Tribes in 1990

The 1990 analysis uses 52 attitude variables from the NZES (n=1,893), clustered at k=5. Tribes are ordered by socioeconomic position, bottom to top.

| Tribe | Size | Left Vote | Right Vote | Non-vote | Other | SES Position |
|-------|------|-----------|------------|----------|-------|--------------|
| Traditional Working Class | 25% | 55% (Lab+Grn+NLP) | 25% (Nat) | 12% | 8% | Working class -- lowest income, ~52% homeownership, ~4% university |
| Educated Progressives | 14% | 67% | 14% | 9% | 10% | Educated precariat -- ~51% homeownership, ~26% university, but low wealth |
| Middle New Zealand | 21% | 51% | 33% | 9% | 7% | Middle class -- ~66% homeownership, ~19% university, average income |
| Social Conservatives | 24% | 15% | 72% | 5% | 8% | Petite bourgeoisie -- ~78% homeownership (56% freehold), ~5% university, asset-rich but income-poor. Older, retired/self-employed, farmers, trades |
| Establishment Right | 15% | 25% | 64% | 5% | 6% | Upper class -- ~78% homeownership, ~16% university, highest income |

1990 "Left" = Labour + Green (former Values Party) + NewLabour Party. "Right" = National. This was the last FPP election.

No survey weights are available for the 1990 NZES.

## 3. Structural Changes: 1990 to 2023

### The progressive coalition fractured along class lines

The 1990 Educated Progressives were a cross-class coalition. University-educated Pakeha liberals and working-class Maori voters sat in the same tribe, united by shared pro-Treaty and pro-redistribution attitudes. By 2023, this coalition had split. The Educated Progressives retained the university-educated, higher-income segment and rose from "educated precariat" (51% homeownership, low wealth despite education) to upper-middle class (22% in the top income bracket). The Precariat Left inherited the working-class, disproportionately Maori/Pacific segment.

This is not merely a relabelling. It reflects a genuine class divergence: the 2023 Educated Progressives and Precariat Left share left-wing attitudes on redistribution and Treaty issues but differ sharply on material circumstances, institutional trust, and rates of political participation.

### The "left but anti-Maori" bloc vanished

The Traditional Working Class (25% in 1990) was economically progressive but opposed to Maori political voice -- a combination that has no 2023 equivalent. This was the largest single cluster in 1990. Its disappearance marks one of the most significant structural shifts in New Zealand political culture over the past three decades.

In parallel, the pro-Treaty constituency grew from one small tribe (14%, the Educated Progressives) to two large ones (the Educated Progressives at 21% and the Precariat Left at 18% -- 39% combined). Support for Maori political voice shifted from a minority position concentrated among the educated to a mainstream position spanning two distinct class groups.

### The petite bourgeoisie became the alienated lower-middle class

The 1990 Social Conservatives were the petite bourgeoisie: asset-rich property owners (78% homeownership, 56% freehold), self-employed, farming, and trades. They had low formal education (5% university) but high material security. They voted National at 72% and barely abstained (5% non-vote).

Their 2023 successors, the Alienated Conservatives, occupy a lower SES position: 30% own outright, 11% in the top income bracket, 19% university. They are defined less by material security than by institutional distrust and cultural resentment. Their non-vote rate quadrupled to 20%. This trajectory -- from propertied conservatism grounded in material interest to alienated conservatism grounded in distrust -- mirrors patterns observed in comparable democracies.

### Non-voting rose dramatically at the bottom

| Tribe (1990 / 2023 equivalent) | 1990 Non-vote | 2023 Non-vote | Change |
|--------------------------------|---------------|---------------|--------|
| Traditional Working Class / Precariat Left | 12% | 19% | +7 |
| Social Conservatives / Alienated Conservatives | 5% | 20% | +15 |
| Middle New Zealand | 9% | 15% | +6 |
| Educated Progressives | 9% | 9% | 0 |
| Establishment Right | 5% | 11% | +6 |

The sharpest increase in non-voting occurred among the lower SES tribes. The Educated Progressives are the only group whose participation rate held steady. This pattern is consistent with a widening class gap in political engagement.

### Continuities

Two tribes are recognisable across both periods with only modest change:

- **Middle New Zealand** remained the median voter tribe in both years -- moderate, swing-prone, and the largest single cluster (21% in 1990, 26% in 2023).
- **The Establishment Right** remained the most homogeneous and ideologically committed tribe in both years -- highest income, most cohesive voting (64% National in 1990, 85% right-bloc in 2023).

## 4. Maori Voters in 1990

### Undersampling

Only 5.5% of the 1990 NZES sample identifies as Maori, compared with approximately 12.5% in the 1991 Census. This undersampling is severe enough to affect clustering: Maori voters lack the sample mass to form a distinct tribe.

In the unweighted five-tribe solution, 62% of Maori respondents fall into the Educated Progressives cluster. Within that cluster, Maori differ from Pakeha on 14 of 52 attitude variables:

- **More pro-Treaty/Maori rights**: MAOSAY z=-1.93 vs -1.19 for Pakeha in the same cluster
- **More distrustful of government**: see government as having too much power
- **More socially conservative**: on smoking, homosexuality, women's equality
- **More economically interventionist**: favour price controls, import controls, compulsory unionism, state ownership
- **Slightly more centrist on self-placement** despite being more left on policy

These differences suggest that Maori voters formed a politically distinct group even in 1990, but were too few in the sample to separate into their own cluster.

### Census-weighted correction

Applying 1991 Census weights (Maori x 2.55, non-Maori x 0.92) to the clustering:

- **At k=5**: the pro-Treaty cluster becomes 51.4% Maori (weighted) -- a majority-Maori tribe. This weighted cluster has a distinctive profile: MAOSAY z=-1.70, lower education (13% university), lower homeownership (60%), and neutral on social liberalism (homosexuality z=+0.09).
- **At k=6**: a similar 53% Maori cluster persists, plus a separate Pakeha liberal cluster (26% university, socially liberal).

With proper demographic correction, a distinctly Maori political tribe is visible in 1990. Its absence from the standard analysis is a sampling artefact, not a reflection of political reality.

### Sub-clustering the Left Progressives

Splitting the 1990 Educated Progressives at k=2 reveals two recognisable sub-groups:

| | University Liberals (45%) | Working-Class Pro-Maori (55%) |
|---|---|---|
| Maori | 8% | 29% |
| University | 31% | 9% |
| Homeownership | 63% | 48% |
| Social liberalism (homosexuality) | z = +1.00 (very liberal) | z = -0.22 (near average) |
| Pro-Maori voice | z = -0.81 | z = -1.46 (more extreme) |
| Redistribution | z = -0.34 | z = -0.63 (more left) |
| Green vote | 14% | 5% |

The primary differentiator is social liberalism (the homosexuality question), not Treaty issues. Both sub-groups are pro-Treaty and pro-redistribution, but the University Liberals are distinctly socially liberal while the Working-Class Pro-Maori sub-group sits near the population average on social issues.

At k=3, a distinctly Maori sub-cluster emerges: 33% Maori, 4% Pacific, 6% university.

These sub-groups foreshadow the 2023 split between the Educated Progressives and the Precariat Left. The class and cultural fault lines within the progressive coalition were present in 1990; they simply had not yet produced separate tribes at the five-cluster level.

## 5. Data Availability Notes

- **1993 NZES** (n=2,251): Available. Weight variable NWT barely adjusts Maori representation (5.4% to 6.2%).
- **1996 NZES** (n=5,015): Available. Contains a deliberate Maori oversample with weights PWT and QWTM. The left-right scale changes from 1-7 to 0-10 in this year. This dataset would be the best candidate for a properly powered 1990s Maori political tribe analysis.
- **1990 NZES**: No survey weights available. Census-based weighting (Maori x 2.55) is the best available correction for Maori undersampling.

## 6. Summary

The New Zealand electorate in 2023 is organised along recognisably similar lines to 1990, but with three structural shifts:

1. The progressive side split along class lines, producing two distinct tribes where there had been one.
2. The anti-Maori left -- a large and politically significant bloc in 1990 -- ceased to exist as a coherent tribe.
3. The conservative side experienced downward class mobility: from propertied security to alienated distrust.

These shifts did not happen suddenly. The sub-cluster analysis of the 1990 Educated Progressives shows that the fault lines within the progressive coalition were already visible 33 years ago. What changed was the political context -- MMP, the Treaty settlement process, rising housing costs, declining union membership -- that eventually made those fault lines into tribe boundaries.
